YouTube Shorts Analytics: What the Numbers Mean

Quick answer

Four numbers tell you almost everything about how a Short is doing: viewed vs. swiped away (how many people stayed instead of swiping past), average view duration (how much of the Short they watched), the retention graph (the exact seconds where they left), and traffic sources (where the views came from). Viewed vs. swiped away decides whether YouTube keeps showing the Short in the feed; average view duration and retention decide whether it keeps being recommended. Check analytics per Short, not just for the whole channel, and change one thing at a time — the hook, the first frame, or the length — so you can see which change moved the number.

Shorts analytics look simpler than long-form analytics, and that is exactly what makes them easy to misread. A Short can collect ten thousand views in a day and still be a weak Short, because in the Shorts feed a view is counted almost as soon as the video starts playing. The numbers that matter are the ones that show whether people chose to stay: did they watch, or did they swipe away? Did they watch to the end, or did they leave after three seconds? And where did those viewers come from — the feed, search, or your channel page? This guide covers the four metrics that matter for Shorts, where to find them, how to read them, and the concrete action each one points to. The number ranges in this guide are general guidance to aim for, not measured benchmarks — every niche behaves differently, and your own trend over time matters more than any single number. Where to find your Shorts analytics

Open YouTube Studio and go to the Content tab, then filter the list by Shorts. The Overview tab shows your channel totals for the last 28 days — Shorts views, subscribers gained from Shorts, and how Shorts compare with your long-form videos — and that is useful for spotting trends. But the real work happens one Short at a time: click a Short, then click Analytics, and you will see that Short's own page, with views, viewed vs. swiped away, average view duration, the retention graph, and traffic sources for that Short alone. Get into the habit of opening the analytics for every Short you publish, two or three days after it goes live and again after two weeks. The first check shows how the Short opened in the feed; the second shows whether YouTube kept showing it. Compare Shorts against each other, not against other channels: your best Short is your own benchmark, and your job is to make the next Short beat it. Shorts numbers move fast — a Short can sit quiet for a week and then take off when the feed picks it up — so do not judge a Short after a single day.

1. Viewed vs. swiped away: did they stay or leave?

Viewed vs. swiped away is the Shorts equivalent of click-through rate, and it is the most important number in Shorts analytics. In the Shorts feed, viewers do not click a thumbnail — they either watch the Short that appears or swipe to the next one. "Viewed" means they stayed and watched; "swiped away" means they moved on without watching. The percentage tells you, at a glance, whether your Short earned its place in the feed. If most people swipe away, the problem is almost always in the first one or two seconds: a slow opening, a frame that does not make clear what the Short is about, or a hook that promises nothing worth staying for. As general guidance, many creators aim for a clear majority of viewers choosing to watch rather than swipe away — but the exact figure depends on the niche, the audience the Short is shown to, and the Short's length, so treat that as a rough target rather than a pass-or-fail line. What matters most is the direction: if you change the first frame or the opening line and the viewed share rises over the next few days, the change worked. Check this number separately for each Short, because a single strong Short can hide a pattern of weak openings across the rest of your channel. The fix for a weak viewed share is always in the opening: start with the payoff, the surprising fact, or the question the Short answers — not with a greeting, a logo, or a slow build-up. 2. Average view duration: how much of the Short did they watch?

Average view duration for a Short is the average number of seconds viewers watched before they left, and it should always be read as a percentage of the Short's length. A 60-second Short with a 30-second average view duration kept viewers for half the video; a 20-second Short with a 15-second average kept them for three quarters — and the shorter Short is the stronger performer, even though its average in seconds is lower. As general guidance, keeping viewers for most of the Short — roughly two thirds or more — is a healthy sign, and a Short watched almost to the end is a strong signal no matter how long it is. If your average view duration is short relative to the Short's length, the problem is usually in the middle: the Short repeats itself, drifts off topic, or takes too long to reach the point it promised. The fix is structural. Cut the weakest five seconds, move the payoff earlier, and end the Short as soon as the point is made — a Short that ends on time earns rewatches, and rewatches count. Shorter is not automatically better — a 50-second Short with great retention earns more total watch time than a 15-second Short — but a long Short that loses half its audience halfway through would have been a better short Short. Before you film your next Short, plan the structure around one idea only: one fact, one tip, one moment. Shorts that try to cover three ideas in 40 seconds usually lose viewers at the second idea.

3. The retention graph: the exact seconds where viewers leave

The retention graph for a Short works the same way as the long-form version, but every second matters more, because the whole video may only last 30 seconds. The curve shows, second by second, what percentage of viewers are still watching. A flat stretch means viewers are engaged; a steep drop means something made them leave — a boring segment, a repeated point, a confusing jump, or a moment where the Short's energy dropped. Look for the biggest drops first. If a third of your audience leaves at the same two-second window, watch that exact part of the Short and ask what went wrong there. Look for rises too: a bump in the curve means viewers rewound or rewatched that part, which tells you what your audience values most — and that part deserves to come earlier in your next Short. As general guidance, aim for a retention curve that falls gradually rather than falling off a cliff — a slow, steady decline is normal, while a sharp drop in the first three seconds is the clearest sign your opening needs work. Use what the curve teaches you in the next Short: move the popular segment earlier, cut the segment where viewers leave, and put the most valuable point in the first half of the video. Because Shorts are short, even a two-second cut can move the average view duration by a visible amount, so small edits are worth making. 4. Traffic sources: where do the Shorts views come from?

Traffic sources tell you how viewers found a Short: the Shorts feed, YouTube search, browse features, your channel page, suggested videos, external sites, or playlists. Each source behaves differently, and each one points to a different action. If most views come from the Shorts feed, the feed is doing the work — and your job is to keep the viewed share high so it keeps doing it. If a Short gets a noticeable share from search, viewers are actively looking for that topic, and you should make more Shorts on the same search topics, using the words searchers actually type. If views come from your channel page or from suggested videos, your existing audience is watching — which means the Short works as a bridge to your long-form videos, so link the two in the Short's description and in a pinned comment. If external traffic is high, your Short is being shared or embedded somewhere — check which sites, and share more Shorts there. And if a Short gets almost no views from any source, it is not being shown in the first place, which usually means the title and description are not giving YouTube a clear idea of who the Short is for. Watch the balance between sources over time: a healthy Shorts channel gets most of its views from the feed, with a small but steady share from search and the channel page. What good numbers look like for a small channel

Small channels should read their Shorts numbers against their own history, not against big creators. A channel with a few hundred subscribers will see smaller view counts, and that is normal — the percentages matter more than the totals. As general guidance, aim for these patterns: a viewed share where most viewers stay rather than swipe away; an average view duration covering most of the Short's length; a retention curve that falls gradually instead of dropping sharply in the first seconds; and at least one traffic source beyond the feed, so the Short is not entirely dependent on a single feed placement. Do not expect every Short to hit all four — most Shorts will be strong on two and weak on the others, and that is fine. The goal is to raise the average over a month, not to make every Short a hit. Keep a simple record: for each Short, write down the views after seven days, the viewed share, the average percentage viewed, and the biggest retention drop. After ten Shorts, the pattern will be obvious — the topics, lengths and openings that work for your audience, and the ones that do not. That record is worth more than any benchmark, because it is measured on your channel, with your audience, in your niche.

Which metrics to act on — and in what order

When a Short underperforms, act on the metrics in this order. First, viewed vs. swiped away: if people are swiping past, nothing else matters, because the Short is not being watched at all. Fix the opening — the first frame, the first line, the promise the Short makes in its first second. Second, the retention graph: if viewers stay for the opening and then leave, find the exact drop and cut or fix that segment. Third, average view duration as a percentage: if viewers leave halfway through, the Short is too long for its one idea — shorten it, or split the idea into two Shorts. Fourth, traffic sources: if the Short has no search traffic at all, the title and description may not match what people search for — rewrite them with the words from our title generator. Do not try to fix everything at once. Change one thing per Short — a hook, a first frame, a length — so you know which change moved the number. And do not delete a Short because its first week was quiet: Shorts can be picked up by the feed weeks later, and a Short with a strong retention curve is worth keeping while it waits for its moment.

Mistakes creators make with Shorts analytics

  • Judging a Short by views alone — in the feed, a view is counted almost as soon as the Short starts, so views without retention mean nothing.
  • Reading average view duration in seconds, not as a percentage — always compare it with the Short's length.
  • Ignoring viewed vs. swiped away — it is the single biggest signal for whether the feed keeps showing the Short.
  • Judging a Short after one day — give a Short at least a week, and often two, before deciding it failed.
  • Comparing your numbers with big channels — their audience, history and distribution are different; compare with your own past Shorts.
  • Checking analytics every hour — the numbers need days to settle, and constant checking changes nothing.
  • Changing everything at once — change one thing per Short so you know which change moved the number.
  • Deleting quiet Shorts — a Short with strong retention can be picked up by the feed weeks later.

Shorts analytics reward one habit above all: looking at the numbers per Short, finding the exact second where viewers leave, and fixing that second in the next Short. The creators who grow on Shorts are not the ones with the best first Short — they are the ones who read what the numbers say about the last Short and change one thing in the next one. Open YouTube Studio today, pick your best-performing Short, and look at its viewed share and its retention graph. Note the moment viewers swipe away and the moment they leave, then make your next Short with those two facts in mind. That single habit, repeated every week, is how a small channel learns what its audience actually wants. Frequently asked questions

What is a good viewed vs. swiped away rate for Shorts?

There is no single good number, because the rate depends on your niche, the audience the Short is shown to, and how long the Short is. As general guidance, many creators aim for a clear majority of viewers choosing to watch rather than swipe away. What matters most is the trend: if a change to your hook or first frame lifts the viewed share, the change worked.

What is a good average view duration for a YouTube Short?

Average view duration for a Short should be read as a percentage of the Short's length, not as a number of seconds. As general guidance, keeping viewers for most of the Short — roughly two thirds or more — is a healthy sign. A Short that is watched almost to the end usually performs better than a longer Short that loses viewers halfway through.

Where do I find YouTube Shorts analytics?

Open YouTube Studio, go to the Content tab, and filter by Shorts. Click a Short, then Analytics, to see its own page: views, viewed vs. swiped away, average view duration, the retention graph and traffic sources for that Short alone. The channel Overview tab shows Shorts totals for the last 28 days, but the video-level page is where the useful detail lives.

Do Shorts views count toward watch time?

Shorts views do not count toward the watch hours needed for the YouTube Partner Program in the same way long-form watch time does — Shorts have their own eligibility path based on Shorts views. Shorts watch time still matters for discovery, because it tells YouTube whether viewers enjoy your Shorts, and that affects how often YouTube shows them in the Shorts feed.
