YouTube Channel Trailer: How to Make One That Converts

Quick answer

A channel trailer that converts does three jobs in under about ninety seconds: it opens with the viewer's need, stated in their own words, so a first-time visitor recognises in the first seconds that this channel is for them; it proves the promise with a fast, honest montage of the channel's real videos, showing the style, the topics and the kind of result a subscriber can expect; and it ends with one clear call to action that names what subscribing delivers and when the next video arrives. It does not introduce the creator at length, it does not list every topic the channel has ever covered, and it does not ask for a subscription before it has earned one. The structure below — hook, promise, proof, schedule, call to action — is the whole trailer, and every second of it should serve one of those five parts. A visitor who lands on a channel page is in a different state of mind from a viewer who lands on a single video. The video viewer has already decided, more or less, to watch something: the title and the thumbnail did their work before the click. The channel-page visitor has decided nothing yet. They arrived because a video, a comment, a search result or a recommendation made them curious about the channel behind it, and they are now doing a quick, almost unconscious check: is this channel for me, is it active, is it worth subscribing to, or should I go back to the video I came from? That check takes seconds, and the channel trailer is the one piece of content made to answer it. The trailer sits at the top of the channel page, it plays for the visitor who has not subscribed, and it is the creator's one chance to speak directly to a stranger who is deciding. Yet most channel trailers waste that chance in the same few ways. They open with a long animated logo, as if the visitor already cares about the brand. They list the creator's history, as if the visitor asked for a biography. They show a slow montage of clips with music and no words, as if the visitor will work out the promise from the pictures alone. Or they are simply an old video pressed into service as a trailer, a full-length tutorial or vlog that a deciding visitor will never watch to the end, and that says nothing about the channel as a whole. A good trailer is none of those things. It is a short, deliberate pitch, made for one viewer — the stranger on the channel page — and built to answer the three questions that stranger is asking: what will I get, is it for someone like me, and why should I subscribe now rather than just watch one video and leave? Answer those three questions, in that order, in a trailer that respects the visitor's time, and the trailer will do the job it exists to do.

What a channel trailer is for — and what it is not for

A channel trailer is a pitch for the channel, not a sample of a single video, and keeping that distinction clear is what makes a trailer work. A single video has to deliver its own result: teach something, answer a question, tell a story, or entertain for its full length. A trailer has to deliver a different result: it has to make a stranger understand the channel well enough, and trust it enough, to subscribe. That means the trailer should show the range of the channel, not the depth of one video. A cooking channel's trailer should show several kinds of dishes, several kinds of videos, and the presenter's way of explaining, so the visitor can see what subscribing buys them over the next few months. A trailer that shows only one recipe, however well filmed, tells the visitor about one video, and the visitor who wanted something else will leave, even though the channel makes exactly what they wanted in its next video. The trailer should also be honest about the channel's real character. If the channel is calm and detailed, the trailer should be calm and detailed. If the channel is fast and funny, the trailer should be fast and funny. A trailer that promises a style the channel does not deliver will win subscriptions that cancel within a week, when the new subscriber's first recommended video feels nothing like the trailer that sold them. The trailer is not for existing subscribers, either. Subscribers do not see it in the same place, and they do not need a pitch for a channel they have already joined. Writing the trailer for the existing audience — in-jokes, references to earlier videos, thanks for support — is writing it for the people who need it least, and it will confuse the stranger it is actually for. Write it for one person: someone who liked one video, clicked through to the channel, and is deciding, right now, whether this channel deserves a place in their subscriptions. Everything in the trailer should be chosen for that person, and nothing should be included that does not help them decide.

The five-part structure that converts

Every trailer that converts follows the same five parts, in the same order, and the order matters as much as the parts themselves. First comes the hook: the opening seconds that name the viewer's need, problem or curiosity, in the words they would use. Second comes the promise: a plain statement of what the channel delivers, who it is for, and how it is different from the other channels covering the same ground. Third comes the proof: a fast montage of real clips from the channel's own videos, chosen to show the range, the style and the quality a subscriber can expect. Fourth comes the schedule and the shape of the channel: what kinds of videos appear, and how often, so the visitor knows what subscribing will put in their feed. Fifth comes the call to action: one clear invitation to subscribe, naming what the viewer will get and pointing to the next video. That order follows the visitor's thinking. The hook earns their attention. The promise gives their attention somewhere to go. The proof makes the promise believable. The schedule makes the promise concrete. The call to action turns belief into a subscription. Rearranging the parts breaks the chain. A trailer that opens with the schedule asks the visitor to care about frequency before they know what the channel is. A trailer that opens with proof, without a promise first, shows clips the visitor has no reason to care about yet. A trailer that asks for a subscription before the proof asks for trust it has not earned. Keep the five parts in order, give each part only the time it needs, and cut anything that does not belong to one of them. The structure is simple on purpose: a stranger has no patience for a clever structure, and a simple structure is one the stranger can follow without thinking, which leaves all of their attention for the promise itself.

Part one: the hook — the first five seconds decide everything

The hook of a trailer is not the same as the hook of a single video, though the two are cousins. A video hook promises the result of that video. A trailer hook promises the result of the channel. It must name, in the first few seconds, the need that brought the visitor to the channel page, and it must do it in the visitor's own language, not in the creator's. A fitness channel for beginners should open with the beginner's situation — wanting to start, not knowing where to begin, worried about doing it wrong — rather than with the creator's qualifications. A history channel should open with the question or the story that its viewers come for, rather than with the period it covers. A channel about making things should open with the finished thing, or the problem the finished thing solves, rather than with the tools. The test for a trailer hook is the same as for any hook: if a stranger heard only those first seconds, with the sound off and the channel name hidden, would they know what the channel is for and whether it is for them? If the answer is no, the hook is doing decoration instead of work. Our hook generator is built for the opening lines of individual videos, and its method applies here too: start with the viewer's problem, their question, or their desired result, and state it as plainly as you can. The difference is only one of scale. A video hook says what this video will do. A trailer hook says what this channel will do, week after week. Write the hook last, if that helps, after the rest of the trailer is clear, because the hook is a summary of the whole promise, and it is easier to summarise a promise that has already been written than to invent one from nothing. Then read the hook aloud, time it, and be ruthless about it: every word that does not name the viewer's need or the channel's answer is a word that delays the decision the visitor came to make.

Part two: the promise — who the channel is for, and what it gives them

The promise is the sentence, or the two or three sentences, that a visitor could repeat to a friend to explain why they subscribed. It should name three things: the audience, the result, and the difference. The audience is who the channel is for, stated narrowly enough to be recognisable. "For anyone who likes cooking" is not an audience; it is everyone, and a promise made to everyone is heard by no one. "For home cooks who want weeknight dinners that taste like they took hours" is an audience, because the home cook who is tired after work recognises themselves in it at once. The result is what the viewer will be able to do, know or feel after watching: cook a better dinner, understand a period of history, fix a common problem, laugh, or learn a skill step by step. The difference is what makes this channel the one to subscribe to, rather than the dozen other channels covering the same ground: the presenter's approach, the level of detail, the honesty about mistakes, the focus on one narrow corner of the topic, or the way the channel explains things for a particular kind of viewer. The promise should be spoken on camera, in the creator's own voice, or shown in text that is easy to read, rather than left to be inferred from the montage. A visitor should be able to state the promise after watching the trailer once, without having to work it out. If you cannot state your own channel's promise in two sentences, the trailer is not the problem; the channel's focus is, and the trailer will be stronger for the thinking you do before you film it. Write the promise down, say it to someone who does not know the channel, and ask them to repeat it back. If they repeat a version of it, the promise is clear. If they repeat the creator's name and a vague topic, the promise needs to be narrower, plainer, or both. The promise is also where honesty does its most important work in the trailer. Do not promise a result the channel does not deliver, a frequency the channel does not keep, or a level the channel does not teach at. A promise that oversells will win a subscription and lose a viewer, and a viewer who unsubscribes after one video is a viewer who will not come back when the channel improves.

Part three: the proof — show the channel, do not describe it

Proof in a trailer is shown, not claimed. A creator who says "my videos are high quality" has made a claim the visitor has no reason to believe. A creator who shows, in twenty seconds, five short clips of real videos — each clip showing a different kind of video the channel makes, each clip looking and sounding like the channel at its best — has given the visitor evidence they can judge for themselves. Choose the clips the way a visitor will judge them: pick clips that show the presenter's face and voice, because the visitor is subscribing to a person as much as to a topic; pick clips that show the range of the channel, because a visitor who only sees one kind of video will assume the channel only makes that kind; and pick clips that show a result, a reaction, or a moment of clarity, because a clip that shows only a process, with no outcome, leaves the visitor wondering what the video was for. Keep each clip very short — a second or two, sometimes three — and let the clips build in energy rather than starting at full speed and staying there. A montage that is all fast cuts and loud music from the first second gives the visitor nowhere to go, and it hides the very thing the visitor is trying to judge: what the videos actually feel like to watch. A montage that starts with the presenter's voice, builds through a few clear examples, and ends on the channel's strongest moment gives the visitor a sense of the channel's rhythm, which is what they will actually live with as a subscriber. Use only clips from videos that are still on the channel and that represent the channel now. An old clip that looks different from the current videos, or a clip from a video that has been removed, will mislead the visitor, and the first video they watch after subscribing will feel like a different channel. If the channel is new and has only a few videos, use what exists and be honest about it: a trailer built from three good videos, promising more of the same, is stronger than a trailer padded with clips that do not represent the channel. And remember that the proof must match the promise. If the promise is about clear explanations, the clips should show explaining. If the promise is about honest testing, the clips should show testing. A mismatch between the promise and the proof tells the visitor the creator does not know what the channel is for, and a visitor who hears that will not subscribe.

Part four: the schedule — make subscribing concrete

The schedule part of the trailer answers a practical question the visitor is asking, whether they know it or not: if I subscribe, what will actually arrive, and how often? A channel that uploads every week should say so, plainly, and should say what kinds of videos arrive in that week. A channel that uploads in series, or in seasons, should say that instead, and should say what the current series is and when the next video in it arrives. The schedule should be a promise the creator can keep, because a schedule that is announced in the trailer and then broken in the first month teaches new subscribers that the channel's promises are not reliable. It is better to promise a modest schedule and keep it than to promise a frequent one and miss it. The schedule part is also the place to name the shape of the channel: the regular series, the occasional formats, and the kinds of videos a subscriber should expect. A channel that makes tutorials, reviews and occasional live streams should name all three, briefly, so the visitor who came for the tutorials knows the reviews exist, and the visitor who came for the reviews is not surprised by them. Keep this part short. The visitor does not need a calendar, and they do not need the history of the upload schedule. They need one clear picture: this kind of video, this often, starting with the video that is coming next. If the channel's schedule is irregular — many channels are, especially small ones — say so honestly, and name what the subscriber will get instead of a fixed day: new videos when they are ready, a series that arrives in full, or a new video every time a topic is worth covering. Honesty about an irregular schedule is better than a false promise of regularity, and a visitor who subscribes knowing the schedule is irregular is a subscriber who will stay. End the schedule part by pointing forward: name the next video, or the series that is starting, so the visitor's decision to subscribe is tied to something specific they will receive, rather than to a general idea of the channel.

Part five: the call to action — one ask, at the end, with a reason

The call to action should be the last thing in the trailer, it should be said once, and it should carry a reason. "Subscribe" on its own is an instruction, not a reason. "Subscribe for a new tutorial every week, and watch the next one here" is a reason, a schedule, and a next step in one sentence. The call to action works best when it is spoken by the creator, on camera, looking at the viewer, because a subscription is a personal decision and a personal ask matches it. A text overlay alone, or a button that appears with no words, asks the visitor to act without telling them why, and most visitors will not. Keep the ask to one thing. A trailer that asks the visitor to subscribe, to watch another video, to follow on another platform, to join a mailing list and to leave a comment is a trailer that asks for everything and gets nothing, because the visitor cannot hold five requests at once and will do none of them. Choose subscribing, and let the end screen or the channel page do the rest. The call to action should also be timed to the moment when the visitor is most ready to act: after the promise, after the proof, and after the schedule have all been given. Asking at the start, before any reason exists, is the most common trailer mistake after the long logo opening, and it fails for the same reason: it asks for trust before it has earned any. If the trailer is well made, the call to action will feel like the natural ending, not like an interruption. The visitor has been told what the channel is for, shown what it looks like, and told when the next video arrives; subscribing is simply the way to make sure they do not miss it. Say it plainly, say it once, and then stop. A trailer that ends cleanly, on the ask, leaves the visitor with the decision in front of them. A trailer that trails on, with extra clips, extra thanks and extra requests after the ask, lets the decision fade, and the visitor leaves the page without having made it.

Length, pace and the first watch: how to keep a stranger watching

Length is not a rule, it is a consequence. A trailer is as long as the five parts need, and no longer. For most channels that is somewhere between about half a minute and about a minute and a half, and a trailer that runs much longer than that should be checked for padding: a second example where one would do, a longer introduction than the visitor needs, a montage that shows the same kind of clip three times, or a call to action that is said twice. Pace matters more than length. A trailer should move: the hook in the first seconds, the promise straight after, the proof clips short and varied, the schedule in a sentence or two, and the call to action before the visitor's attention runs out. That does not mean the trailer should be frantic. A calm channel should have a calm trailer, and a fast channel should have a fast one, but both should move forward without lingering. The way to test pace is to watch the trailer as a stranger would: play it once, without stopping, and notice the moment your attention wanders. That moment is where the trailer is too slow, too repetitive, or too vague, and it is the place to cut. A useful discipline is to film more than you need and cut hard. Film the promise in three versions, film the call to action in two, gather twice as many proof clips as the trailer will hold, and then choose only the strongest. The trailer that results will feel confident, because every second in it earned its place, and the seconds that did not were removed. Sound matters to pace too. The trailer should be understandable with the sound on, and the music, if there is any, should support the voice rather than compete with it. A visitor who cannot hear the promise over the music will leave, and a visitor who watches with the sound off should still be able to follow the trailer from the pictures and any on-screen text. Keep the on-screen text short and large enough to read on a phone, because many channel-page visitors are on a phone, and text that is readable on a large screen may be unreadable on a small one. Finally, watch the trailer on the channel page itself, as a visitor would, before you publish it. A trailer that works in an editing program and fails on the channel page — because the first frame is uninviting, or the title and description around it promise something different — has not been tested where it will be used.

Worked examples: three trailers, three kinds of channel

It is easier to see the structure working than to be told about it, so here are three worked outlines, each for a different kind of channel, each following the same five parts. The first is for a beginner cooking channel. The hook is the viewer's evening: "You are tired, you have half an hour, and you still want dinner to taste good." The promise follows: "This channel is for home cooks who want weeknight dinners that taste like they took hours, with recipes that use ordinary ingredients and one pan." The proof is a montage of four dishes, each shown finished and being served, with the presenter explaining one step in each clip, so the visitor hears the teaching as well as seeing the food. The schedule is one sentence: "A new recipe every week, plus a longer weekend cook on the first Saturday of the month." The call to action names the next video: "Subscribe, and start with this week's recipe, which is linked below." The second outline is for a channel that explains how things work, for curious viewers. The hook is a question: "Why do some bridges sway, and why do engineers let them?" The promise is: "This channel answers one question about the built world in every video, in plain language, with drawings and real examples." The proof shows the presenter drawing, a real bridge, and a moment where a complicated idea becomes clear in a single sentence. The schedule is: "A new question answered every two weeks, in a series that builds from the everyday to the surprising." The call to action points to the series: "Subscribe, and the next question is the one viewers asked for most." The third outline is for a channel about restoring old furniture. The hook is the finished piece, shown before the work: "This chair was left outside for two winters. Watch what it becomes." The promise is: "Restorations, start to finish, with the mistakes left in and the methods explained, for people who like making old things useful again." The proof is a fast sequence of stages — stripping, repairing, finishing — each shown for a second or two, ending on the finished chair in a real room. The schedule is: "A full restoration every month, and a shorter repair in between." The call to action is: "Subscribe to see the next restoration from its first day." Notice what all three have in common: the hook is the viewer's need, the promise is two sentences, the proof shows the work rather than describing it, the schedule is one sentence, and the call to action names the next video. The topics are different, but the structure is the same, and that is why the structure is worth learning: once it is learned, it can be filled with any channel's promise, and it will work for any channel whose promise is real.

Filming the trailer: what to shoot, what to reuse, what to avoid

A trailer can be filmed from scratch, built from existing clips, or made from a mixture of both, and each approach has a place. Filming from scratch gives the most control: the creator can speak the promise directly to the camera, in the channel's usual setting, with the lighting and sound the channel normally uses, and the trailer will look and feel like the channel from its first frame. Building from existing clips is faster, and it guarantees that the proof clips are real, because they are taken from videos the visitor can watch, but it risks a trailer that feels like a compilation, with no single voice holding it together. The mixture is often the best of both: film the hook, the promise, the schedule and the call to action directly to camera, in one short session, and cut in the proof clips from existing videos around them. The direct-to-camera parts give the trailer its voice and its structure, and the existing clips give it its evidence. Whichever approach is chosen, the trailer should be filmed and edited to the standard of the channel's best videos, not to the standard of a quick announcement, because the visitor will judge the channel by the trailer's quality. That does not mean expensive equipment or elaborate effects. It means clean sound, steady framing, good light, and an edit without mistakes: no clip that starts mid-sentence, no jump in volume between the voice and the music, no text with a spelling error, and no clip that lingers a second too long. Avoid the three filming habits that weaken trailers most. The first is the long logo opening: a visitor who has not decided yet does not need to be shown a logo for several seconds, and a logo opening tells them the trailer is about the brand rather than about them. Keep any logo to the final frame, or to a corner, where it belongs. The second is the apology or the disclaimer: "I am not very good at this", "this is my first trailer", or "sorry about the sound" all teach the visitor to doubt the channel before they have seen it. If the sound is bad, fix the sound and refilm. Do not apologise for it in the trailer. The third is the montage with no voice at all: a trailer that shows clips over music, with no spoken promise, asks the visitor to guess what the channel is for, and most visitors will not guess. They will leave. Speak the promise, show the proof, and let the two work together. That is the whole craft of filming a trailer, and it is within reach of any creator who can film a normal video.

Title, description and thumbnail: the packaging around the trailer

The trailer does not stand alone on the channel page. It stands beside the channel's name, its description, and the trailer video's own title, description and thumbnail, and all of those should agree with each other. The trailer video's title should say what it is, plainly: "Welcome to" followed by the channel name, or "Channel Trailer" with the channel's promise, is clearer than a clever title that a visitor cannot parse. The description of the trailer video should carry the promise in words, with links to the videos and playlists the trailer mentions, following the method in our description guide and the patterns in our worked description examples. A visitor who reads the description should find the same promise they heard in the trailer, and the same next step. The thumbnail for the trailer video matters, even though the trailer is shown on the channel page, because the trailer can also be found in search and in recommendations, where the thumbnail is the first thing a viewer sees. The thumbnail should show the creator's face, or the channel's most recognisable subject, with a short piece of text if any, following the principles in our thumbnail ideas guide. The channel description, the short text under the channel name, should carry the same promise as the trailer, in one or two sentences, because a visitor who reads the description and then watches the trailer should hear the same promise twice, not two different promises. Agreement between all of these — trailer, title, description, thumbnail and channel description — is what makes a channel page feel trustworthy. A visitor who hears one promise in the trailer, reads another in the description, and sees a third in the thumbnail will conclude, reasonably, that the creator does not know what the channel is for, and will leave. Check the whole page together, as a visitor would, before the trailer goes live: read the channel description, watch the trailer, read the trailer's description, and look at the thumbnail, and ask whether all four say the same thing, in the same voice, about the same channel. If they do, the page is doing its job. If they do not, fix the one that is out of step, and the trailer will convert better for it.

Measuring whether the trailer works — without chasing numbers

A trailer's job is to convert visitors into subscribers, and the honest way to judge it is to watch what visitors do after watching it, over time, rather than to judge it by a single day's figures. The signs of a working trailer are practical, not technical: new subscribers mention the trailer, or arrive soon after the channel page is visited; the trailer is watched to the end by a reasonable share of the visitors who start it; and the subscribers who arrive stay, watch the next videos, and behave like the audience the trailer promised. The signs of a trailer that is not working are equally practical: visitors start the trailer and leave within seconds, which usually means the hook is not naming their need; visitors watch the trailer but do not subscribe, which usually means the promise is unclear, the proof is unconvincing, or the call to action is missing; or new subscribers leave quickly, which usually means the trailer promised something the channel does not deliver. Each of those signs points to a different part of the trailer, and each can be fixed by reworking that part rather than by remaking the whole trailer. If the hook is the problem, rewrite the first seconds. If the promise is the problem, narrow it. If the proof is the problem, choose stronger clips. If the call to action is the problem, say it once, at the end, with a reason. Resist the temptation to judge the trailer by comparing it with other channels' trailers, or by chasing a single figure. A trailer for a narrow channel will convert a smaller share of a smaller number of visitors than a trailer for a broad channel, and that is not a failure; it is the price of being specific, and specificity is what makes a channel worth subscribing to. Judge the trailer by whether it brings the right viewers — the viewers the channel was made for — and whether those viewers stay. That judgement takes weeks, not hours, and it should be made by watching the channel's new subscribers and their behaviour, not by refreshing a single page. When the trailer is changed, change one part at a time, and give the change time to show its effect, because changing everything at once makes it impossible to know what helped. A trailer is a living part of the channel page, not a monument, and the creator who revisits it, improves one part, and watches the result will end up with a trailer that converts far better than the creator who films it once and never looks at it again.

Mistakes that stop a trailer converting

  • Opening with a logo or a long intro — the visitor has not decided to care about the brand yet, and a slow opening spends their attention before the promise is made.
  • Listing the creator's history — the trailer is about the viewer's need, not the creator's biography, and a biography will be skipped.
  • Showing a montage with no spoken promise — clips alone ask the visitor to guess what the channel is for, and most visitors will not guess.
  • Promising everything the channel has ever covered — a promise made to everyone is heard by no one, and a list of topics is not a promise.
  • Asking for a subscription before giving a reason — an early ask feels like an interruption, and it asks for trust that has not been earned.
  • Using old clips that no longer look like the channel — the first video after subscribing will feel like a different channel, and the subscriber will leave.
  • Asking for five things at once — subscribe, watch, follow, join and comment cannot be held in mind at once, and none of them will happen.
  • Leaving the trailer unchanged when the channel changes — a trailer that describes a channel you no longer make will attract the wrong viewers and disappoint the right ones.
  • Making the trailer a full-length video — a deciding visitor will not watch a long video to learn about a channel, and a long trailer is a video pretending to be a pitch.
  • Promising a schedule the channel does not keep — a broken schedule teaches new subscribers that the channel's promises are unreliable, and they will not stay to see it fixed.

A channel trailer is the shortest pitch a creator will ever make, and it is also the most important one, because it is the pitch made to a stranger who is deciding whether to become a subscriber. Make it for that stranger, and for no one else. Open with their need, in their words. State the promise plainly: who the channel is for, what it gives them, and how it is different. Prove the promise with real clips that show the range and the quality of the channel. Name the schedule honestly, and point to the next video. Ask once, at the end, with a reason, and then stop. Keep it short, keep it honest, and keep it current, and the trailer will do the quiet, steady work it exists to do: turning visitors who were curious about one video into subscribers who stay for the channel. Start today, with the promise written down in two sentences. Film the direct-to-camera parts in one session, gather the strongest proof clips from the videos that already exist, and cut the trailer to the five parts in order. Then watch it as a stranger, on the channel page, with the sound on and with the sound off, and ask the only question that matters: if I had never seen this channel before, would I subscribe? If the answer is yes, publish it. If the answer is not yet, fix the part that made you hesitate — the hook, the promise, the proof, the schedule or the call to action — and watch it again. The trailer you publish after that check will be a trailer that converts, because it was built the way a visitor decides: quickly, honestly, and with one clear next step. Frequently asked questions

How long should a YouTube channel trailer be?

Short enough that a visitor can watch it to the end without deciding to leave, and long enough to say who the channel is for, what it delivers, and why it is worth subscribing to. For most channels that means somewhere between about half a minute and about a minute and a half. A trailer that runs much longer than that starts to feel like a video in its own right, and a visitor who has not decided yet will usually leave before it finishes. A trailer that is only a few seconds long rarely says enough to earn a subscription. Aim for a length you can fill with one clear promise, a little proof, and one call to action, with nothing left over.

What should a channel trailer say in the first few seconds?

It should say, in the words of the viewer you want, what the channel gives them and who it is for. The first seconds are the hook: the moment a visitor decides whether to keep watching or to leave the channel page. Open with the result, the problem, or the question the channel answers, rather than with your name, a long introduction, or a request to subscribe. A viewer who hears their own need described in the first seconds will stay to hear the rest. A viewer who hears a biography will leave, because the trailer is about them, not about you.

Should a channel trailer ask viewers to subscribe?

Yes, once, near the end, after the trailer has given the viewer a reason to subscribe. The call to action should name what the viewer will get by subscribing — the kind of videos, how often they appear, and what the next one will be — rather than simply saying subscribe. Asking at the start, before any reason has been given, asks the viewer to commit before they know what they are committing to. Asking at the end, with the promise fresh in their mind, turns the subscription into the natural next step of the trailer, not an interruption of it.

How often should I update my channel trailer?

Update it whenever the channel's promise changes, and review it at least once or twice a year even if it has not. If the topics change, the audience changes, the upload schedule changes, or the channel's style changes, the trailer should change with it, because a trailer that describes a channel you no longer make will attract the wrong viewers and disappoint the right ones.
