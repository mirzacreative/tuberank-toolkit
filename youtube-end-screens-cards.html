YouTube End Screens and Cards That Keep Viewers Watching

Quick answer

The last twenty seconds of your video and the small cards that appear during it are the cheapest watch-time tools YouTube gives you. An end screen in the final 20 seconds should offer exactly one next video — the one most related to the video the viewer just watched — plus a subscribe button. Cards work best as one or two well-timed links placed at the moment a viewer would naturally wonder about a related topic. The goal is simple: never let a viewer finish your video with nowhere to go, because a viewer with nowhere to go leaves your channel.

Most creators spend hours on the title, the thumbnail and the first thirty seconds — and then let the video end with a fade to black and a silent invitation to leave. That ending is a leak. Every video you publish is a chance to earn a second video view from the same person, and the end screen is the tool built for exactly that job. Cards do the same work in the middle of the video, catching viewers at the moment they think of a question your other video already answers. Used well, these two small features turn single-video viewers into session viewers, and session viewers are the audience YouTube recommends your channel to more often. This guide shows how to set them up, what to promote, and the mistakes that waste them.

Why viewers leave at the end — and how to stop it

Watch what happens when a video ends without an end screen. YouTube shows a grid of suggested videos, most of them from other channels, and the viewer clicks whatever looks most interesting. You did all the work of earning that viewer's attention and then handed them to a competitor at the finish line. An end screen changes that moment. Instead of a grid of other people's videos, the viewer sees your next video, large and clearly recommended by you, plus a subscribe button. The viewer still has a choice, but now the easiest choice is to stay with you. The same logic applies to cards. When you mention a topic mid-video and a card appears offering the video that covers it in depth, the viewer does not have to search for it later — they click now, and your watch time keeps growing. End screens and cards are not tricks. They are signposts, and a channel without signposts loses viewers at every junction.

End screens: the rules you need to know

End screens can appear only in the last 20 seconds of a video, and YouTube requires the video to be at least 25 seconds long. You can add up to four elements: a video or playlist, a subscribe button, a channel link, or a link to an approved external site. In practice, two elements are enough for most videos: one video or playlist, and one subscribe button. Four elements crowd the screen and split the click. The end screen needs a clean background to sit on — a plain outro slide, a still frame of you on camera, or a simple animated background works. Do not put important visuals, text or faces where the end screen boxes will cover them. Leave the last twenty seconds visually calm, keep talking or use a short outro line, and let the end screen elements do their job. Plan the outro while you script the video, not after you edit it: if you know the last twenty seconds are reserved, you will not accidentally put your best line or your key visual there.

What to put in your end screen

The single most important decision is which video to promote. The answer is almost always the video most related to the one the viewer just watched. A viewer who finished your video about budget microphones is far more likely to click your video about microphone setup than your newest upload about lighting. Relevance beats recency. If your newest video happens to be on the same topic, promote that — it gets the freshness bonus and the relevance bonus together. Otherwise, promote your best-performing related video, or better still, a playlist that starts with the most relevant video, because a playlist click can turn into two or three more views. For a series, always promote the next episode. The subscribe button goes in the remaining slot, and it should be placed where it does not cover your face or your outro text. If your video is a tutorial, consider promoting the follow-up tutorial or the tool you mentioned. If it is a review, promote the comparison video. The test is simple: if a viewer enjoyed this video, which of your videos would they most want next? That is the one to promote. Cards: the quieter tool that works mid-video

Cards are the small "i" icon that appears in the top-right corner of the player, expanding into a link when the viewer taps it. You can add up to five cards per video, but one or two well-timed cards will always beat five cards that pop up every minute. The best moment for a card is the exact second you mention something your other video covers in depth. Say "I made a full video about this" and the card appears at that moment — the viewer feels helped, not interrupted. Cards can link to videos, playlists, channels, or approved external links, and they work on every device. A common mistake is placing cards in the first thirty seconds, when the viewer has only just decided to watch this video. Let them settle in first. Another mistake is using cards to push a video that has nothing to do with the current topic — viewers ignore irrelevant cards, and ignored cards teach the viewer to ignore the icon entirely. Timing and placement that actually work

For end screens, use the full 20-second window whenever you can. A five-second end screen is easy to miss, especially on mobile, where the viewer may already be reaching for the next video. Twenty seconds gives the viewer time to read the title of the recommended video, decide, and click. Start the end screen exactly when your main content ends — not before, or you cut off your own ending, and not after a long silence, or the viewer has already left. For cards, the timing rule is relevance: place the card within a few seconds of the moment you mention the linked topic, and never place two cards closer than a minute apart. Keep the total number of cards low on short videos — a three-minute video with five cards feels like an ad break. On longer videos, space them out so each one arrives when the viewer's interest in that sub-topic is highest. Finally, check your work on a phone. Most viewers watch on mobile, and an end screen that looks clean on a desktop edit can cover a face or a caption on a small screen. Play the last thirty seconds on your own phone before you publish.

Pairing end screens with your call to action

The end screen and your spoken call to action should say the same thing. If the end screen promotes your video about thumbnails, your outro line should be "if you want your thumbnails to get more clicks, watch this next" — not "don't forget to like and subscribe and check out my other stuff". One clear instruction beats three vague ones. Say the name of the recommended video out loud, point towards it if you are on camera, and keep the subscribe ask short: "subscribe if you want the next one". The subscribe button on the end screen does the rest. This pairing is also why the outro should be scripted, not improvised. Write the last two sentences of every video before you film, so the call to action matches the end screen element exactly. Mistakes that waste your end screens and cards

  • No end screen at all — the viewer finishes, sees other channels' videos, and leaves.
  • Promoting your newest video regardless of topic — relevance beats recency; an unrelated recommendation gets ignored.
  • Four crowded elements — too many choices means no choice gets clicked. One video plus subscribe is enough.
  • End screen over faces or key text — the boxes cover your outro and look messy.
  • Cards in the first thirty seconds — the viewer has barely started watching; let them settle in first.
  • Five cards in a short video — constant pop-ups feel like advertising and train viewers to ignore the icon.
  • A different call to action from the end screen — if you say "watch my vlog" but the end screen shows a tutorial, the viewer does neither.
  • Never checking on mobile — most viewers are on phones, and end screens that look fine on desktop can cover the wrong thing on a small screen.

End screens and cards take about ten minutes to set up per video, and they work on every video you have already published too. Go back to your five best-performing videos, add an end screen pointing to the most related video, and add one card where you mention a topic you covered elsewhere. That is an afternoon of work that keeps earning watch time for months, because those videos already get views. Then make the end screen part of your upload checklist: title, description, thumbnail, end screen, cards, publish. A channel that always gives the viewer a next step keeps viewers longer, and viewers who stay longer are the ones YouTube keeps recommending. Start with the video that already brings you the most traffic, set its end screen today, and let the rest of your catalogue follow.

Frequently asked questions

How long should a YouTube end screen be?

The last 20 seconds of your video is the usual end screen window, and YouTube allows end screens in the final 5 to 20 seconds. Use the full 20 seconds when you have a clear next video to recommend, because a longer window gives more viewers time to notice and click the end screen elements.

How many cards can I add to a YouTube video?

You can add up to five cards per video. Use them sparingly — one or two well-timed cards beat five cards that pop up every minute. Place a card at the moment in the video where the linked video or resource is most relevant, so the viewer feels helped rather than interrupted.

Should my end screen promote my newest video or my best video?

Promote the video most related to the one the viewer just watched, not simply your newest upload. A related video converts far better than a random one. If your newest video is on the same topic, promote that; otherwise promote your best-performing related video, or a playlist that starts with the most relevant video.

Do end screens and cards actually increase watch time?

Yes, when they are used well. End screens turn a video ending into a next-video click, which adds a whole session of watch time instead of letting the viewer leave YouTube or drift to a competitor. Cards work the same way mid-video, pointing viewers to the exact video that answers the question they just thought of.
