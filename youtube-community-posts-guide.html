YouTube Community Posts That Grow Engagement

Quick answer

A YouTube Community post keeps a channel in touch with its viewers in the days between uploads. The posts that work best do one small job well: they ask a real question, show something the viewer has not seen, or give a good reason to watch an older video again. A poll that lets viewers choose the next topic, a photograph from behind the scenes, a short update about what is being made, or a shared video with a question attached will each do that job, provided the post is written for the people who already follow the channel rather than for strangers. Post once or twice a week, answer the comments the posts draw, and let the posts point back to the videos, because the videos remain the main work of the channel. This guide is about the space between uploads. For earning the click on a video itself, read our click-through rate guide and our thumbnail ideas guide; for keeping viewers inside a video once they click, read our audience retention guide; and for what happens when a video ends, read our end screens and cards guide and our playlists guide.

Every channel has the same quiet problem, and it is not the problem most creators talk about. The talked-about problem is the upload: the title, the thumbnail, the opening seconds, the editing. The quiet problem is the gap. A channel that uploads once a week is silent for six days out of seven. A channel that uploads once a month is silent for most of the month. In that silence, viewers do not sit still and wait. They watch other channels, they forget the name of a channel they liked, and when the next video finally arrives it must win their attention all over again, from cold. Community posts exist for that gap. They are short posts a channel can publish on its Community tab — a poll, a photograph, a short written update, a shared video — that keep the channel present without requiring a finished video. They are smaller than a video, quicker to make, and easier for a viewer to answer: a vote takes a second, a short comment takes a minute, and a glance at a photograph takes no effort at all. That smallness is their strength. A viewer who would not sit down to watch a ten-minute video on a busy evening may still vote in a poll, and that vote is a small act of staying connected. Over weeks, those small acts add up to a habit: the viewer checks the channel, the viewer recognises the creator's name, and the next upload arrives in front of someone who has not forgotten the channel. This guide explains how to use Community posts well, what kinds of posts suit which purpose, how often to post, and how to tell whether the posts are working — without inventing statistics about how viewers behave in general, and without promising growth that no post can guarantee. Everything here is judged by the one test a creator can trust: what happens on their own channel, over several weeks, when they publish posts of each kind and watch the response.

What a Community post is, and where viewers meet it

A Community post is a short publication that appears on a channel's Community tab, the part of the channel page set aside for posts that are not videos. Depending on the viewer and on how they use YouTube, a post may also reach them beyond the channel page, for example in their feed or through a notification, but a creator should not assume that every subscriber will see every post. The safe way to think about a Community post is as a message left where interested viewers can find it: the people most likely to see it are the people who already visit the channel, who follow it closely, or who have shown interest in its posts before. That audience is smaller than the audience for a video, and it is also warmer. These are the viewers who already know the channel, who have watched before, and who are the most likely to answer, to vote, and to watch the next upload. A Community post is therefore not a way to reach strangers. It is a way to look after the viewers a channel already has, and to keep them close between uploads. The kinds of post available to a channel depend on the channel itself: the features a creator sees when they open the post composer, and whether the Community tab is available to them at all, can vary, and a creator whose channel does not yet show the tab should check the current requirements in YouTube Studio rather than taking any figure in an older guide as current. This guide describes the post types in general terms — polls, images, written updates and shared videos — and the advice applies whichever of those types a channel can publish. A creator should work with what their own channel offers, and should not build a routine around a post type they cannot publish. The other thing to understand early is that a Community post is public and permanent in the same way a video is. A hasty post, a careless claim, or a promise made in a poll cannot easily be taken back once viewers have answered it, and a post that announces a video that never arrives will be remembered. The standard for a post should be the same as the standard for a video: only publish what is true, only promise what will be delivered, and only ask a question whose answer the creator is willing to act on.

Why the gap between uploads matters more than it looks

The gap between uploads matters because attention is not stored. A viewer who enjoyed a video on a Saturday has not promised to remember the channel on the following Saturday. In the days between, that viewer watches other videos, follows other channels, and forms new habits, and the channel that was vivid on Saturday can become a half-remembered name by the next weekend. This is not a failure of the video. It is the ordinary way attention works, and it affects every channel, large and small. A channel that uploads daily suffers from it least, because each new video refreshes the viewer's memory. A channel that uploads weekly or monthly suffers from it most, because the refresh comes rarely, and each upload must do the work of reintroducing the channel as well as delivering its subject. Community posts soften that problem. They do not replace the refresh that a video gives, but they keep the channel's name in front of the viewer in a lighter way: a poll on a Wednesday, a photograph on a Friday, a short update on a Sunday. None of those asks the viewer for ten minutes. Each of them reminds the viewer that the channel is alive, that the creator is working, and that something is coming. The second reason the gap matters is that it is where a channel learns. A video takes hours or days to make, and a creator who waits until the video is finished to learn what viewers want must wait a long time between lessons. A poll takes minutes to publish and returns an answer within hours. A question in a post can reveal, before a video is made, which of two topics viewers care about, which wording they use for a problem, or which example would help them most. A creator who uses the gap to ask will make better videos, because the next video will be built on an answer rather than a guess. The third reason is trust. A channel that only speaks when it has a finished video to promote can start to feel like a shop window: polished, distant, and only interested in the viewer's click. A channel that also speaks between uploads — that shows the work in progress, that admits a delay honestly, that thanks viewers for an answer — feels like a person, and viewers are more forgiving of a late video from a person they trust than from a shop window they do not. The gap, used well, is where that trust is built, one small post at a time.

The four kinds of post, and the job each one does

Community posts fall into four broad kinds, and each kind does a different job. A poll asks a question with a fixed set of answers and invites a vote. Its job is to learn and to involve: it tells the creator what viewers prefer, and it tells the viewer that their preference matters. A poll works best when the choice is real — when the creator genuinely does not know which answer will win, and will act on the result. A poll with an obvious answer, or with answers the creator has already decided between, wastes the viewer's vote and teaches them that the next poll is not worth answering. An image post shows a photograph or a graphic. Its job is to show rather than tell: the work in progress, the place, the thing being made, the comparison between two versions. An image post works best when the image itself carries the interest, and the words around it are short. A long explanation attached to a photograph will be skipped; a photograph that needs a long explanation was probably not the right photograph. A written update is a short piece of text: news, a note about a delay, a thank-you, a thought about the subject. Its job is to keep viewers informed and to keep the channel's voice present. A written update works best when it says one thing clearly and in the creator's own voice, rather than trying to say everything at once. A shared video post presents a video — the channel's own, or occasionally another channel's — with a short introduction. Its job is to give an older video a second audience, or to introduce a new video to the followers who check the Community tab. A shared post works best when the introduction adds a reason to watch that the title alone does not give, as the final section of this guide explains. Most channels will use all four kinds over time, and the balance between them will settle according to the channel's subject. A channel about making things will lean on images. A channel about questions and explanations will lean on polls. A channel with a loyal following will lean on written updates. The mistake is to use only one kind, week after week, until viewers stop noticing it, or to use a kind for a job it cannot do — a poll to announce a delay, for example, or a long written update to show a visual comparison. Match the kind to the job, keep each post short, and let the variety itself keep the tab worth visiting.

Polls that teach the creator something real

A good poll begins before the question is written, with a decision the creator actually has to make. The best poll questions are the ones a creator is already asking themselves: which of these two topics should the next video cover, which example would help a beginner most, which thumbnail direction feels clearer, what time of day suits the viewers who watch live, or which part of a subject deserves a video of its own. A poll built on a real decision has two virtues. First, the creator will use the answer, which means the poll is honest work rather than decoration. Second, the viewer can feel that the decision is real, because the options are genuinely different and the result will be visible in what the channel does next. A poll built on a question the creator has already answered — a question whose answer is obvious, or a question asked only to fill the tab — has neither virtue, and viewers learn quickly to skip it. The options matter as much as the question. Two to four options is usually enough. Each option should be a real choice, stated in the words a viewer would use, and the options should be genuinely different from one another rather than small variations on the same thing. A poll that offers three versions of the same answer is not a choice; it is a trick, and viewers will treat it as one. The wording of the question should be short and plain. A poll question that needs a paragraph of explanation is a question that belongs in a video, not in a poll. Ask the question, give the options, and add at most one short sentence of context — why the choice matters, or what will happen with the result. Then, and this is the part most creators miss, close the loop. When the poll has run its course, publish the result: a short post saying what won, what the channel will do about it, and when. A poll whose result is never reported teaches viewers that their vote went nowhere. A poll whose result changes the next video teaches viewers that their vote shaped the channel, and those viewers will vote again, and will watch the video they helped choose. The creator should also read the comments on a poll, not only the votes. The votes say which option won. The comments say why, and the why is often more useful than the result: a viewer who explains their vote in a sentence has told the creator how they think about the subject, and that thinking is the raw material for the next video's opening, its examples, and its wording. Keep a note of the poll results and the reasons viewers gave, in the same place where video ideas are kept — our content calendar guide and our video ideas guide both cover how to turn a viewer answer into a planned video — and let the polls become a steady, cheap form of research that runs between uploads.

Images and behind the scenes: showing the work, not the polish

An image post works when it shows something a viewer cannot see anywhere else. The finished video is polished by design: it shows the result, edited, in its best light. The Community tab can show the other side of the work: the desk where the video was made, the notes for a script, the two thumbnail drafts that did not win, the location before the filming began, the tool or the material in use, or the mistake that taught the creator something. Viewers respond to those images because they answer a curiosity that the finished video cannot answer: what is this channel actually like, behind the finished product? A creator who only ever shows polish will be admired, but a creator who sometimes shows the work will be trusted, and trust is what brings a viewer back between uploads. The images that work best are honest and specific. A photograph of the actual workspace, however untidy, will do more than a clean stock-style image, because it is evidence that the work is real. A comparison image — the same shot before and after an edit, two versions of a thumbnail side by side, a first draft next to the finished version — will do more than either image alone, because it gives the viewer something to judge, and judging is a form of taking part. A photograph that invites a question will do more than a photograph that only invites admiration: a picture of a workbench with an unfamiliar tool, a picture of a half-finished project, or a picture of a choice the creator has not yet made will draw comments in a way that a finished, perfect picture will not. The caption should be short. One or two sentences is usually enough: what the image shows, and the one question or thought that goes with it. A long caption turns an image post into a written update with a picture attached, and the viewer who came for the picture will not read it. The creator should also think about what the image promises. An image of a project half-built promises that the finished project will appear, and the creator should be ready to publish it, or to explain honestly why it will not appear. An image of a new piece of equipment promises that the equipment will be used and reviewed, and a viewer who sees it will remember. Show only what the channel is willing to follow through on, and the images will build expectation rather than disappointment. Written updates: short, honest, and in the creator's own voice

A written update is the simplest kind of Community post, and the easiest to get wrong, because its simplicity tempts a creator to say too much, too vaguely, or in a voice that is not their own. The written updates that work share three qualities. They are short: one piece of news, one thought, one thank-you, said in a few sentences. They are honest: a delay explained plainly, a change of plan admitted without excuses, a result reported as it happened rather than as the creator wishes it had happened. And they are in the creator's own voice: the same voice the viewer hears in the videos, written down, rather than a formal announcement voice that sounds as if it came from someone else. A viewer who reads a written update should feel that the creator is speaking to them directly, and a viewer who feels spoken to will answer. The occasions for a written update are the ordinary occasions of running a channel. A video will be late, and the creator says so, says why in a sentence, and says when it will arrive. A video has done well, and the creator thanks the viewers who watched it and names the comment that helped. A subject has changed — a tool has been updated, a rule has changed, a place has closed — and the creator tells viewers what it means for the next video. A question has come up again and again in comments, and the creator answers it briefly and promises the fuller answer in the next video. Each of those is one post, not a list of posts, and each of them keeps the channel present without asking the viewer for much. The occasions to avoid are the ones that ask the viewer for something while giving nothing: a post that only says a video is coming, with no detail, no date and no reason to care; a post that repeats the same announcement a channel has already made; or a post that apologises at length for a silence without saying what will change. Viewers are generous with creators who are plain about a problem, and impatient with creators who are vague about one. The other habit worth building is to write the update as if it were the beginning of a conversation, not the end of one. End with the one thing the creator would like to know from viewers, or the one thing viewers should watch for next, rather than with a closing line that leaves nothing to answer. A post that ends with a question will draw comments. A post that ends with a full stop will draw a glance. And when viewers do answer, answer them back, as the replies section below explains, because a written update that starts a conversation the creator then ignores will teach viewers not to start the next one.

Bringing an older video back: shares that add a reason to watch

Every channel has videos that deserved more viewers than they received. A video published on a busy week, a video whose title did not quite land, a video that answers a question viewers are asking now but were not asking then — these videos sit in the channel's back catalogue, still useful, still accurate, and largely unseen. A Community post can give such a video a second introduction, and for a channel with a growing back catalogue this is one of the most useful things the Community tab can do. The key is that the post must add a reason to watch that the video's own page does not already give. Simply sharing a video with its title repeated adds nothing: the viewer has already seen the title, in the feed or on the channel page, and has already decided, once, not to watch it. The post must give the viewer a new reason, written for the followers who already know the channel. That reason can be a question the video answers, stated in the viewer's own words. It can be a change since the video was published: the subject has moved on, a new example has appeared, or the advice in the video has been tested and still holds. It can be a connection to something current: a viewer asked about this subject in a comment this week, a poll chose it, or a new video builds on it. It can be a detail that did not fit in the video, or a correction, or a viewer's comment that showed the video in a new light. Any of those gives the viewer something they did not have when they first passed the video by, and any of them is a fair reason to look again. The choice of which video to share matters too. Share the video that answers the question viewers are asking now, not the video the creator most wishes had done well. Share the video that fits the season, the news, or the conversation already happening in the comments. And share one video at a time, with a post that is about that video, rather than sharing several videos in a row, which will read as a catalogue and be ignored as one. Our playlists guide covers the longer-term version of this work — grouping videos so that one video leads naturally to the next — and a shared post can point to a playlist as well as to a single video, where the playlist itself answers a viewer's need. For a new video, the same rule applies with one addition: share it with a line written for the followers, not copied from the description. The description is written for strangers who find the video in search. The Community post is written for people who already follow the channel, and it should sound like it: a sentence about why this video, why now, and what the creator most wants viewers to notice in it. That second introduction will not reach everyone, but it will reach the viewers most likely to watch, and it costs only a few minutes to write.

Replies and comments: the post is only the beginning

A Community post that receives no reply from its creator is a message sent into a room and then abandoned. The post itself is only the beginning of the engagement it is meant to grow. The engagement happens in what follows: the votes, the comments, and above all the creator's replies to those comments. A viewer who votes in a poll and then sees the creator act on the result has taken part in the channel. A viewer who leaves a comment and receives a real reply — a reply that answers what they actually said, rather than a copied thank-you — has had a conversation, and a viewer who has had a conversation will come back for the next one. A viewer who leaves a comment and hears nothing will conclude, reasonably, that the post was not really an invitation at all. The routine for replies should be simple and sustainable. Check the comments on a new post within a day, while the conversation is still alive, and answer the comments that ask a question, correct a point, or add something worth acknowledging. Answer in the creator's own voice, briefly, and by name where the comment gives one. Do not try to answer every comment with a long reply: a short, specific answer to the comments that need one, and a visible presence in the thread, will do more than a long reply to one comment while the rest go unanswered. Where a comment raises a question that deserves a video, say so, and note the question for the next video — that is how the Community tab feeds the channel's ideas, and viewers who see their question become a video will stay loyal for a long time. Where a comment is critical, answer it calmly and briefly, without arguing and without sharing anything private: acknowledge the point, correct the fact if a fact is wrong, and move on. A calm reply to criticism, visible to every viewer who reads the thread, does more for a channel's reputation than any number of positive posts, because it shows how the creator handles disagreement. Where a comment is spam, or abusive, or plainly not part of the conversation, remove it or ignore it according to the channel's usual practice, and do not let it set the tone of the thread. The same care applies to the comments on the videos themselves, and the habit built on Community posts — quick, specific, human replies — will serve the whole channel. A creator who replies well on the Community tab and never on videos will look inconsistent, and a creator who replies well everywhere will build the kind of audience that watches because they like the creator, not only because they liked one video.

A weekly routine a small channel can actually keep

The best Community routine is the one a creator will still be keeping in three months, and that means it must be small, planned, and fitted to the upload schedule rather than added on top of it. A workable pattern for a channel that uploads once a week is this: one poll early in the week, asking a question that will shape the next video or the one after it; one image or behind-the-scenes post in the middle of the week, showing the work in progress; and, on the day a video is published, one shared post that gives the video its second introduction. A channel that uploads less often can add one written update in the empty weeks, so that no week passes without a sign of life. A channel that uploads more often may need only one Community post a week, because the videos themselves keep the channel present. The pattern should be written down, in the same calendar where uploads are planned — our content calendar guide shows how to plan a month of videos and posts together, so that a poll in one week feeds a video in the next — and the posts should be prepared in batches where possible. A creator who sits down once a week for half an hour can draft the week's posts, choose the images, and write the poll questions, and then publish them through the week without having to invent each one on the day. Batching also prevents the most common failure of a Community routine: the post that is written in a hurry, on a tired evening, and says nothing worth reading. The routine should also have a rule for busy weeks. When a video is late, the routine does not stop: it becomes more important, because a short written update explaining the delay, honestly and briefly, is what keeps viewers patient. When the creator is away, one post saying so, with a date for the return, will do more than silence. And when a post performs poorly — few votes, no comments — the routine does not change in panic. One quiet post is not evidence that the tab has stopped working. Several quiet weeks, with different kinds of posts tried, is the evidence to act on, and the action is to change the kind of post, not to post more of the same. Keep the routine steady, keep the posts short, and keep the measure of success modest: a channel that posts twice a week, answers its comments, and closes its polls will have a Community tab that feels alive, and that aliveness is the whole point.

What not to post: spam, repetition, and promises made too soon

Community posts fail in a small number of predictable ways, and each of them is easy to avoid once it is named. The first is over-promotion: a tab that contains nothing but links to videos, merchandise, or other channels will be read as advertising, and viewers will stop visiting it. A post that links to a video is fine when the post itself gives the viewer something — a question, a reason, a detail — and it is spam when the post is only the link. The test is simple: if the link were removed, would the post still be worth reading? If the answer is no, the post is not ready. The second failure is repetition: the same announcement, the same wording, the same kind of post, week after week, until viewers learn to skip the tab entirely. Variety is not decoration here; it is the reason to keep visiting. Rotate the kinds of post, rotate the subjects, and never publish the same post twice with only the date changed. The third failure is the promise made too soon. A poll that promises a video on a subject the creator has not started, an image that promises a project that may not happen, or an update that promises a date the creator cannot keep will all be remembered, and each broken promise costs more trust than the post gained. Promise only what is already planned, and where a plan is uncertain, say so: a post that says a video is being considered, and asks whether viewers want it, is honest and useful, while a post that says it is coming next week, when it is not, is neither. The fourth failure is the post written for the wrong audience: a post that explains the channel from the beginning, as if to a stranger, will bore the followers who already know it, and a post full of in-jokes that only long-time viewers understand will confuse the newer ones. The Community tab's audience is mainly followers, so write for them, but write plainly enough that a newer viewer can follow. The fifth failure is the post that asks for engagement without earning it: a question with no real choice, an image with nothing in it, an update with no news. Viewers give their attention where they have received something first, and a post that gives nothing will receive nothing. Before publishing any post, read it once as a viewer would, and ask the only question that matters: would I stop, vote, or reply to this, and if not, what would make me? The answer to that question, applied honestly, will improve more posts than any list of rules.

Measuring whether Community posts are working

A creator cannot judge Community posts by a single number, and should not try. The posts do several jobs — keeping the channel present, learning what viewers want, bringing older videos back, starting conversations — and each job has its own evidence. The evidence for presence is steadiness: are posts going out regularly, are they being seen, and are the same viewers returning to them? The evidence for learning is in the poll results and the comments: did the poll give a clear answer, did the comments explain why, and did the next video use what was learned? The evidence for older videos is in the traffic to the shared video: did visits to that video rise in the days after the post, and did viewers who arrived from the post watch it through? Our analytics guide explains how to read a channel's own figures, and our Shorts analytics guide covers the short-form side of the same question, and both should be used with the same caution this guide has used throughout: compare a channel with itself, over time, rather than with other channels or with figures quoted in general terms. The evidence for conversation is the simplest to see and the hardest to fake: are viewers commenting, are they answering one another, and are they mentioning the posts when they comment on videos? A thread where viewers talk to each other, as well as to the creator, is a community in the proper sense, and it is worth more than any single figure. The creator should keep a simple record: for each post, its kind, its subject, the votes or comments it drew, and whether it sent viewers to a video. After a month, that record will show which kinds of post the channel's own viewers answer, and the routine should shift towards those kinds, while still keeping some variety. What the record should not be used for is panic. A quiet week is normal. A post that fails is information, not a verdict. Change one thing at a time — the kind of post, the question, the time of day — and watch the next few posts before judging the change. And remember what Community posts are for: they support the videos, they do not replace them. If the posts are taking time away from making videos, or if the channel's videos are getting worse while its posts get better, the balance is wrong, and the posts should be cut back. A channel is judged, in the end, by its videos, and the Community tab's job is to make sure the viewers are still there, still interested, and still listening when the next video arrives. Mistakes that waste the Community tab

The mistakes worth naming are the ones creators repeat without noticing. Posting only when a video is ready, and leaving the tab silent for the rest of the month, so that the tab looks abandoned whenever a viewer checks it. Publishing a poll and never reporting the result, so that viewers learn their votes go nowhere. Asking a question the creator has already answered, so that the poll feels like a formality. Sharing a video with no added reason, so that the post is only a repeated title. Writing long updates that bury the one piece of news a viewer needed, so that the news is never read. Promising a video, a project, or a date before it is certain, and then going quiet when the promise cannot be kept. Replying to no comments, so that the conversation the post started dies in the first day. Copying the kind of post another channel publishes, without asking whether this channel's viewers would answer it. And treating a quiet post as a failure of the whole tab, and either posting far more often in panic or abandoning the tab altogether, when the honest response is to change one thing and watch. Avoid those, keep the routine small and steady, answer the viewers who answer, and close the loops the posts open, and the Community tab will do the quiet job it exists for: keeping a channel alive in the days between uploads, and keeping its viewers ready for the next video.

Frequently asked questions

How often should I post on the Community tab?

There is no single frequency that suits every channel, and this guide does not invent one. A workable starting point for most small channels is one or two Community posts a week between uploads, kept up for several weeks so viewers learn that the channel is active even when no video has gone out. What matters more than the number is steadiness: two posts a week, every week, will do more for a channel than ten posts in one week followed by silence for a month. Watch your own channel's response — the comments, the votes and the visits to the videos you link — and adjust from there. If posting starts to take time away from making the videos themselves, post less. The videos are still the main work; Community posts support them.

Do Community posts help a channel grow?

Community posts do not replace videos, and no honest guide can promise growth from them alone. What they can do is keep a channel present in a viewer's mind between uploads, give viewers a low-effort way to take part — a vote, a short reply, a reaction — and bring an older video back in front of people who missed it. That presence matters most for channels that upload weekly or less often, where a viewer can otherwise forget a channel exists between videos. Judge Community posts by what you can see on your own channel: do they draw comments, do they send viewers to the video you linked, and do viewers mention them when they comment on a new video? If the answer is yes over several weeks, the posts are earning their place in the routine.

What kind of Community post gets the most engagement?

No type wins for every channel, and a post that works for one audience can fall flat for another, so the honest answer is to test on your own channel. In general, posts that ask the viewer to do one small, clear thing tend to draw more response than posts that only announce something: a poll with a real choice in it, a question with two or three honest answers, or an image that invites a comparison will usually draw more votes and comments than a plain announcement. Posts that show something the viewer has not seen before — a behind-the-scenes photograph, a draft, a choice between two thumbnails — also tend to do well, because they give the viewer something only this channel can show. The best evidence is your own: publish each type a few times, compare the votes and comments each one drew, and publish more of what your own viewers answered.

Should I share every new video as a Community post?

Sharing a new video is a reasonable use of a Community post, but sharing every video in the same way, with the same wording, will teach viewers to ignore the posts. A better habit is to share a video only when the post adds something the video page does not: a question the video answers, a choice the viewer helped make, a detail that did not fit in the video, or a reason this particular video matters to this particular viewer. If the post only repeats the title, it adds nothing, and a viewer who has already seen the video in their feed gains nothing from seeing it again as a post. Use Community posts to give the video a second introduction, written for the people who already follow the channel, and let the video itself do the rest of the work.
