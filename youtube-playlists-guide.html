YouTube Playlists: Organise Videos That Keep Watching

Quick answer

A playlist that keeps viewers watching is built for one viewer journey, not for every video a channel has ever made. Start by deciding what the viewer should be able to do, know or finish after watching the playlist in order: learn a skill step by step, follow a series from the first episode, or explore one topic without searching for each video separately. Give the playlist a name that states that result in the viewer's own words, write a description that says who it is for and what comes first, put the strongest and most welcoming video first, order the rest so each video answers the question the previous one raises, and link to the playlist itself — not to a single video — from end screens, descriptions and the channel page, so the first click starts the sequence. A playlist is not a folder for old videos. It is a path through a channel, and a path only works when every video on it belongs there and the order itself teaches something. Most channels treat playlists as housekeeping. A new video is published, it is dropped into a playlist named after the topic, and the playlist is never looked at again. Months later the channel has a dozen playlists, each holding a mixture of videos in the order they happened to be published, with names that mean something to the creator and nothing to a viewer: a series name nobody searches for, a vague topic word, or a playlist that holds forty videos because nobody ever took any out. A viewer who opens one of those playlists finds no path. The first video may be the newest rather than the best place to start, the second may assume knowledge the first never gave, and the third may be about something else entirely. The viewer does what any sensible person does when a path leads nowhere useful: they leave, they search for the next video themselves, and the channel loses the very thing a playlist exists to give it — a second, third and fourth view from the same person, in the same sitting, without the viewer having to decide what to watch next. A good playlist removes a decision. When a video ends inside a playlist, the next video starts, and the viewer who was enjoying the first video does not have to choose, search or scroll. They only have to stay. That is why playlists matter far more than their reputation as a filing system suggests. They are the channel's way of answering, in advance, the question every satisfied viewer asks at the end of a good video: what should I watch next? A channel that answers that question with a playlist it has planned, ordered and named for the viewer will keep viewers longer than a channel that answers it with a grid of suggestions chosen by someone else. This guide builds playlists the second way: as deliberate paths, planned before the videos are added, named for the viewer, ordered so the sequence itself makes sense, and promoted in the places where a viewer is actually deciding what to watch next.

Why playlists keep viewers when single videos cannot

A single video, however good, ends. The moment it ends is the most dangerous moment in a channel's relationship with a viewer, because the viewer is satisfied, their question is answered or their curiosity is fed, and they have no reason to stay unless the channel gives them one before the feeling fades. The platform will fill that moment with suggestions, and most of those suggestions will be from other channels, because the platform's job is to keep the viewer on the platform, not on one channel. A playlist changes what happens in that moment. When the video is playing as part of a playlist, the next video is already chosen, it is a video from the same channel, and it begins without the viewer having to make a fresh decision. The viewer who would have left after one video stays for a second, and the viewer who stays for a second is far more likely to stay for a third, because each video in a well-ordered playlist earns the next one: it raises a question, it promises the answer, and the answer is the next video in the sequence. That is the whole mechanism, and it is worth understanding clearly, because it explains every decision in this guide. Playlists do not keep viewers by tricking them. They keep viewers by removing friction at the exact moment when friction would otherwise end the session, and by replacing a choice the viewer has no reason to make well — picking the next video from a grid of strangers' thumbnails — with a choice the creator has already made well, in advance. Playlists also help viewers who arrive in the middle. A viewer who finds the third episode of a series through search, or through a recommendation, needs a way back to the start and a way forward to the next episode. A series playlist gives them both in one click, and it gives the creator a place to point from every episode: the playlist link in the description, the end screen that promotes the playlist, the spoken line that says the whole series is collected in one place. Without that, the mid-series viewer watches one episode out of order, enjoys it, and leaves, because finding the rest feels like work. With it, the same viewer can watch the series the way it was meant to be watched, and the creator's earlier work gets the audience it was made for. Finally, playlists help the channel itself. A channel organised into a few clear playlists tells a new visitor, at a glance, what the channel covers, how deep it goes, and where a beginner should start. A channel with no playlists, or with a dozen vague ones, tells the same visitor nothing, and the visitor must work out the channel's shape from a long list of individual videos. The visitor will not do that work. They will watch the one video they came for and leave. The playlist is therefore not only a viewing tool. It is the channel's table of contents, and a channel without a table of contents is a book nobody can navigate.

The four kinds of playlist every channel needs

Not all playlists do the same job, and a channel that uses one kind for every purpose will end up with playlists that do none of them well. There are four kinds worth building, and most channels need all four, in small numbers, kept separate. The first is the series playlist. It holds one series, in episode order, from the first episode to the latest, and nothing else. Its job is to let a viewer follow the series as it was made, and its order must never be changed to put a popular episode first, because a series watched out of order is a story told backwards. When a new episode is published, it goes at the end of the series playlist, and the earlier episodes keep pointing forward to it. The second is the topic playlist. It collects the channel's videos about one subject, in the order a viewer should watch them, starting with the clearest introduction and moving towards the deeper or more specific videos. Its job is to answer a search: a viewer who wants to learn about one topic should be able to open the playlist and find a course, not a pile. The third is the beginner playlist, sometimes called a start-here playlist. It holds a small number of videos — often fewer than ten — chosen to give a new viewer the channel in miniature: the best introduction to the channel's subject, the most useful tutorial, the video that best shows the creator's way of explaining, and the video that answers the question new viewers ask most. Its job is to convert a curious visitor into a regular viewer, and it should be the playlist promoted on the channel page and in the channel's introduction. The fourth is the format playlist: videos of one kind, such as question-and-answer videos, short explainers, live recordings, or behind-the-scenes videos, collected so a viewer who likes that format can find more of it without wading through everything else. Format playlists are the least important of the four, and a channel should only build them when it has enough videos in a format to fill one honestly. A channel with three question-and-answer videos does not need a question-and-answer playlist. It needs three more question-and-answer videos. The mistake to avoid, with all four kinds, is the catch-all playlist: a playlist named after a broad word, holding every video that mentions that word, in no particular order, growing forever. Catch-all playlists are the ones viewers abandon, because the viewer cannot tell from the name what watching it will give them, and cannot tell from the order where to start. If a playlist cannot be described in one sentence — who it is for, and what they will be able to do after watching it — it is not a playlist yet. It is a folder, and folders do not keep viewers watching.

Planning a playlist before adding a single video

The best time to plan a playlist is before the videos in it exist, or at least before they are added, because a playlist planned around the videos that already exist will inherit their accidents: the order they were published in, the gaps in the sequence, and the videos that do not belong. Planning starts with the viewer and the outcome. Write down, in one sentence, who the playlist is for and what they will get from watching it in order. A sentence like "beginners who want to set up their first home studio, in the order they need to buy and connect each piece of equipment" is a plan. A sentence like "all my studio videos" is not. The sentence decides what belongs in the playlist and what does not, and it decides the order: the video that answers the first question comes first, the video that answers the second comes second, and so on, until the outcome is reached. Next, list the videos the playlist needs, including the ones that do not exist yet. A topic playlist that jumps from choosing equipment to advanced techniques, with no video in between explaining how to set the equipment up, has a gap, and the gap is where viewers will leave to search for the missing step somewhere else. Naming the gap is useful: it becomes the next video to make, and when it is made it slots into the playlist in its proper place, rather than being added at the end where it will never be found. Then decide the first video with special care, because the first video is the playlist's promise. A viewer who opens a playlist watches the first video first, and judges the whole playlist by it. The first video should be welcoming, clear, and representative: it should show the creator at their best, it should not assume knowledge the viewer does not have, and it should end by pointing to the next video in the sequence. The last video matters too, though for a different reason. A playlist that ends with a video that points nowhere leaves the viewer at the same cliff edge a single video leaves them at. The last video in a playlist should point to another playlist, to the channel's best next step, or to subscribing, using the end screen and the spoken call to action described in our end screens and cards guide. Finally, decide the name and the description before adding videos, because the name and description are what the viewer sees when deciding whether to start, and a playlist named after the planning process — a working title, a series code, or a word only the creator understands — will never be started by a stranger. The planning takes an hour for a whole channel's playlists. The playlists it produces will do their work for years.

Naming playlists so viewers choose them

A playlist name is a promise, and viewers judge it the way they judge a video title: quickly, and against the other choices in front of them. The name should say what the viewer will get, in the words the viewer would use to ask for it. A playlist called "Beginner Guitar: Your First Ten Lessons, In Order" tells a beginner exactly what they will get, how much of it there is, and that the order matters. A playlist called "Guitar Stuff" tells them nothing, and a playlist called "Series 2" tells them even less, because it assumes they already know what Series 2 is. The name should also be honest about scope. A playlist that promises a complete course and holds four videos will disappoint, and a playlist that promises four videos and holds forty will overwhelm. The number in the name, where a number is used, should match the number of videos, and it should be updated when the number changes, or left out entirely if the playlist will keep growing. The description carries the detail the name cannot. It should say, in two or three sentences, who the playlist is for, what it covers, and what order to watch it in, and it should name the first video and the outcome in plain words. The description is also the place for the words a viewer might search for, used naturally: the topic, the level, and the result, written as sentences rather than as a list of keywords. A description that is only a list of keywords reads like a filing label, and a viewer who reads it will conclude the playlist was made for a search engine rather than for them. A description that reads like a short, honest introduction — "This playlist is for beginners who have never edited a video. Watch it in order: the first video covers the setup, the second covers the first edit, and by the last video you will have finished a complete short video of your own" — reads like an invitation, and invitations are started. Keep the name short enough to be read in a search result or on a phone, keep the description plain, and check both by reading them as a stranger: if you did not know the channel, would you know what this playlist gives you, and would you want it? If the answer is no, the name is describing the videos. Rewrite it to describe the viewer.

Ordering: the sequence is the teaching

Order is where most playlists fail, because the default order — newest first, or the order videos were added — is almost never the order a viewer should watch in. A viewer who wants to learn something needs the foundation first, the next step second, and the advanced work last. A viewer following a series needs episode one first, even if episode seven is the most popular. A viewer exploring a topic needs the clearest introduction first, even if it is the oldest video on the channel. Ordering a playlist therefore means thinking about the viewer who has never seen the channel before, and putting yourself in their place at each step: after watching this video, what will they want, what will they need, and what will they be ready for? The practical method is simple. Lay the videos out in the order a viewer should watch them, read the sequence as a story, and check for three faults. The first fault is the missing step: a video that assumes something no earlier video in the playlist has explained. Fill it, either by making the missing video or by moving a video that does explain it earlier in the order. The second fault is the repeated step: two videos that cover the same ground back to back, so the viewer feels they have already watched the second one. Separate them, or remove the weaker one from this playlist — a video can live in another playlist, and a playlist is stronger for what it leaves out than for what it includes. The third fault is the dead end: a video in the middle of the playlist that points to nothing, so the sequence stalls. Every video in a playlist should, in its final minute, point forward to the next one, either in words or through the end screen, as described in our end screens and cards guide. Check the order again after every few new videos, because a playlist is a living thing: a new video may belong in the middle of an existing playlist, not at the end, and an old video that no longer represents the channel should be taken out, however popular it once was. A playlist whose order has been thought about, and kept thought about, is a course. A playlist whose order is the order of publication is a diary. Viewers finish courses. They do not finish diaries.

Descriptions, thumbnails and the playlist page itself

A playlist has a page of its own, and that page is where a viewer decides whether to start, so it deserves the same care as a video page. The playlist's thumbnail is usually taken from one of its videos, and the video chosen should be the one that best represents the whole playlist: the clearest image, the most welcoming face, the subject the playlist is about. If the first video's thumbnail is weak, the playlist will look weak, and the fix is to improve the first video's thumbnail — following the method in our thumbnail ideas guide — rather than to accept a playlist page that nobody clicks. The description, as already described, should introduce the playlist in two or three honest sentences, and it should be updated when the playlist changes: when a new video joins it, when the order changes, or when the outcome it promises changes. A description that describes a playlist of six videos when the playlist now holds twenty is a description that misleads, and a viewer who is misled at the playlist page will not trust the videos inside it. The playlist page also shows the number of videos and the total running time, and those numbers shape the viewer's decision. A very long total running time can put a viewer off starting, which is another reason to keep topic playlists focused and to split a large topic into two playlists — a beginner playlist and an advanced playlist, for example — rather than one playlist that takes a whole day to watch. A viewer who finishes the beginner playlist and wants more is a viewer who will start the advanced one gladly. A viewer who is shown the whole mountain at once may not start at all. Finally, review the playlist page as a viewer would, on a phone, because that is how many viewers will meet it: is the name readable, does the description say what the playlist gives, does the first video look inviting, and is the order clear? If any of those fails, fix it before promoting the playlist anywhere, because promoting a playlist page that does not convince will waste the promotion and teach viewers to ignore the next one.

Creating, adding and maintaining playlists in practice

Creating a playlist is the easiest part of the work, and it should be done deliberately rather than in passing. Create the playlist from the channel's own library, give it its final name and its description before adding videos, and set it to public only when it is ready to be found — a playlist that is published half-built, with a working title and three videos in the wrong order, will be found by viewers who judge it as finished. Add videos one at a time, in the order they should be watched, checking after each addition that the sequence still reads as a path. When a new video is published, decide at once which playlists it belongs to, and add it in its proper place in each one, rather than letting it sit unfiled until a later tidy-up that never happens. A video added to the end of a series playlist is in the right place. A video added to the end of a topic playlist may belong in the middle, after the introduction and before the advanced work, and adding it at the end because that is the default will quietly break the sequence. Maintenance is the part that is most often skipped, and it is the part that keeps playlists working. Once a season, or whenever a batch of new videos has been published, open each playlist and read it as a viewer: does the first video still represent the channel, does every video still belong, is the order still the order a newcomer should watch in, and does the description still describe what is there? Remove videos that no longer belong, including videos that were once popular but now teach an outdated method or show a style the channel has left behind. A playlist that holds a video the creator would be embarrassed to show a new viewer is a playlist that is showing it to new viewers every day. Rename a playlist when its scope changes, and update the description at the same time, so the name and the contents never disagree. And retire playlists that have served their purpose: a playlist for a finished challenge, a playlist for a series that will not continue, or a playlist whose topic has been split into two better ones should be removed or merged, not left to confuse the channel page. A channel with five excellent playlists is easier to navigate, and easier to trust, than a channel with twenty neglected ones. The work of maintenance is small — an hour every few months — and it is what separates a channel whose playlists are a system from a channel whose playlists are a habit.

Pairing playlists with end screens, cards and descriptions

A playlist that is never linked to will rarely be found, however good it is, because viewers do not go looking for playlists. They find them through the videos they are already watching. The most effective place to promote a playlist is the end screen of every video in it, and the promotion should point to the playlist itself, not to the next single video, where the choice is between the two. Promoting the playlist means the viewer who clicks starts the sequence, and the sequence does the work of keeping them. Promoting a single video means the viewer watches one more video and reaches the same cliff edge again. The end screen element that promotes a playlist should be paired with a spoken line that names the playlist and its promise, as described in our end screens and cards guide: a line such as "the whole series is in a playlist, linked on screen and below — start from the beginning and it will take you through every step" gives the viewer a reason to click, and tells them what clicking will do. Cards play a supporting role. A card placed at the moment a video mentions a topic the playlist covers can point to the playlist, but it should be used sparingly — one card per video, at the most relevant moment — because cards that appear constantly train viewers to ignore them. The video description should carry the playlist link too, in the first few lines, where a viewer who reads descriptions will see it, and it should say what the playlist is, not merely that it exists: "Watch the full beginner series in order" with the link is an invitation, while "Playlist" with the link is a label. The same link belongs in the descriptions of the videos that lead into the playlist from outside it: a popular standalone video on a topic should point, in its description and its end screen, to the playlist that collects the deeper work on that topic, because the popular video is the doorway and the playlist is the room behind it. Our description guide and our worked description examples show how to place such links without cluttering the description. The test for all of this is the viewer's journey: starting from any video on the channel, can the viewer reach a playlist that collects the next step, in one click, without searching? If the answer is yes for the channel's best videos, the playlists are doing their job. If the answer is no, the playlists exist but are not connected, and a playlist that is not connected is a library with no doors.

Channel page sections: showing the right playlist first

The channel page is the one place where a visitor sees the channel as a whole, and the playlists shown there should be chosen the way a shopkeeper chooses a window display: the best, the most welcoming, and the most useful first. A new visitor should see, without scrolling far, the beginner or start-here playlist, the channel's best series, and the topic playlist that represents the channel's main subject. Those three sections answer the visitor's three questions: where do I start, what is this channel's best work, and does it cover the thing I came for? A channel page crowded with every playlist the channel has ever made answers none of those questions, because the visitor cannot tell which playlist matters. Keep the channel page to a small number of sections, ordered by the visitor's needs rather than by the creator's pride: the playlist that helps a stranger first, then the playlist that shows the channel's depth, then the rest. The sections should be checked as part of the seasonal maintenance already described, because a channel page that still shows a finished series at the top, months after it ended, while the current series is buried below, is telling visitors about the channel's past rather than its present. A visitor who lands on a channel page after watching one video should be able to find, in seconds, the playlist that continues that video's subject. If they cannot, the channel page is not doing its job, however beautiful it looks. The trailer, covered in our channel trailer guide, and the channel page sections should agree with each other: the trailer promises a journey, and the sections show where that journey starts. When they agree, the visitor's path is clear from the first seconds on the page. When they disagree — a trailer that promises a beginner course and a channel page that shows no beginner playlist — the visitor will conclude the promise was empty, and will leave. Agreement between the trailer, the sections and the playlists is what makes a channel page feel trustworthy, and trust is what turns a visitor into a subscriber. Measuring whether a playlist works — without chasing numbers

A playlist's job is to keep viewers watching, and the honest way to judge it is to watch what viewers do after starting it, over time, rather than to judge it by a single day's figures. The signs of a working playlist are practical. Viewers start the playlist and watch more than one video from it before leaving. Viewers arrive at later videos in the sequence through the playlist, rather than only through search or recommendations. The first video in the playlist keeps bringing new viewers into the sequence, and the videos in the middle of the sequence are watched by people who arrived through the playlist, not only by people who found them separately. The signs of a playlist that is not working are equally practical, and each points to a different fault. Viewers start the playlist and leave after the first video, which usually means the first video does not point forward clearly, or the second video does not deliver what the first one promised. Viewers start the playlist in the middle and never reach the start, which usually means the playlist is not linked from the videos that lead into it, or its name does not say it should be watched in order. Viewers never start the playlist at all, which usually means its name or description does not promise anything they want, or it is not promoted from the videos and the channel page where they would find it. Each of those signs can be fixed by reworking one part — the first video, the links, or the name — rather than by rebuilding the whole playlist. Resist the temptation to judge a playlist by comparing it with other channels' playlists, or by chasing a single figure. A playlist for a narrow topic will be started by fewer viewers than a playlist for a broad one, and that is not a failure; it is the price of being specific, and specificity is what makes a playlist worth starting. Judge the playlist by whether it serves the viewers it was made for, and whether those viewers watch more than one video from it. That judgement takes weeks, not hours, and it should be made by watching how viewers move through the sequence, not by refreshing a single page. When a playlist is changed, change one thing at a time — the name, the order, or the first video — and give the change time to show its effect, because changing everything at once makes it impossible to know what helped. A playlist is a living part of the channel, not a monument, and the creator who revisits it, improves one part, and watches the result will end up with playlists that keep viewers far longer than the creator who builds them once and never looks at them again. Mistakes that stop playlists working

  • One catch-all playlist per topic — a broad folder with no order and no promise, which viewers open once and never finish.
  • Newest-first ordering — the default order, which puts the latest video first even when it is the worst place for a newcomer to start.
  • Names that describe the creator's filing system — series codes and vague topic words that mean nothing to a stranger deciding what to watch.
  • Descriptions that list keywords — a filing label where an invitation should be, which tells the viewer the playlist was made for a search engine.
  • Promoting single videos instead of the playlist — an end screen that earns one more view, when the playlist would have earned a whole session.
  • Never removing outdated videos — a playlist that keeps showing new viewers work the channel has moved beyond.
  • Too many playlists on the channel page — a window display with everything in it, which tells a visitor nothing about where to start.
  • Playlists that are never linked from anywhere — a library with no doors, which viewers cannot find however good it is.
  • Starting a playlist with the weakest video — a first video that does not represent the channel, which judges the whole sequence before it begins.
  • Leaving gaps in the sequence — a jump from the basics to the advanced work, with the missing step sending viewers to search elsewhere.

A playlist is the simplest retention tool a channel has, and it is also the most neglected, because it looks like filing. It is not filing. It is the channel's answer to the question every satisfied viewer asks: what next? Answer that question deliberately, and the channel will keep viewers it would otherwise have lost at the end of every video. Build four kinds of playlist, and no more than the channel needs: a series playlist in episode order, a topic playlist that reads like a course, a short beginner playlist that shows the channel in miniature, and a format playlist only when a format has enough videos to fill one honestly. Name each playlist for the result the viewer wants, describe it as an invitation, order it so each video earns the next, and link to the playlist itself from end screens, descriptions and the channel page. Then maintain it: review the order, remove what no longer belongs, fill the gaps with new videos, and retire what has served its purpose. Start today, with the playlist that matters most — the one a new viewer should start with. Plan it on paper first: the viewer, the outcome, the videos in order, the name and the description. Then build it, link it from the videos that lead into it, and put it first on the channel page. Then watch how viewers move through it, fix the one part that makes them stop, and watch again. The playlist that results will do the quiet, steady work playlists exist to do: turning one view into a session, and a session into a subscriber. Frequently asked questions

How many videos should a YouTube playlist have?

A playlist should have as many videos as the topic honestly needs, and no more. A short playlist of four or five closely related videos that a viewer can finish in one sitting will usually serve a viewer better than a playlist of fifty videos on loosely related topics, because the viewer can see the end and trust that every video in it is worth watching. A series playlist should hold every episode in order, however many that is, because a viewer who finds episode three should be able to find episodes one and two without searching. A topic playlist can be longer, provided every video in it is genuinely about that topic and the best videos come first. The test is not the number. It is whether a viewer who opens the playlist would be glad, after watching two or three videos from it, that they did not have to search for each one separately.

Should YouTube playlists be public?

Keep the playlists you want viewers to find set to public, because a playlist that is not public cannot be found in search, cannot be recommended, and cannot be linked to from an end screen in a way that helps a new viewer. Use unlisted or private settings for playlists that are still being built, for playlists made for one client or one collaborator, or for a personal collection of reference videos that was never meant for viewers. When a playlist is ready — the order is right, the title and description say what it contains, and every video in it belongs there — make it public and link to it from the videos it collects, from the channel page, and from any related guide or post. A public playlist is a small library. A private one is a drawer.

Can one video be in more than one playlist?

Yes, and most videos should be, because one video usually serves more than one viewer journey. A tutorial can belong in a series playlist in episode order, in a topic playlist with the other tutorials on the same subject, and in a beginner playlist that collects the starting points for new viewers. Each placement serves a different viewer: the series follower, the topic searcher, and the newcomer. The limit is relevance. A video placed in a playlist it does not belong to will disappoint the viewer who opened that playlist expecting one thing and found another, and a playlist full of loosely related videos will be abandoned. Add a video to every playlist it genuinely serves, and to no playlist it does not.

How do I get viewers to start a playlist?

Give them a reason to start it, and make starting it easy. Name the playlist for the result the viewer wants, not for the videos it contains, and write a description that says who it is for and what watching it in order will give them. Link to the playlist, not to a single video, from end screens, from descriptions, and from the channel page, so the first click starts the sequence rather than a single video. Mention the playlist in the videos themselves, at the moment when the viewer would most want the next step, and say plainly what comes next in the sequence. A viewer who knows the playlist will answer their next three questions will start it. A viewer who is only told that a playlist exists will not.
