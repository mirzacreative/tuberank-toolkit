YouTube Description Examples That Get Clicks

Quick answer

A description that gets clicks does three jobs in its first lines: it names the exact result the video delivers, it names the viewer it is for, and it tells that viewer what to do next. Everything after that — chapters, links, extra detail — supports those first lines; it never replaces them. The examples below show that pattern working for tutorials, reviews, vlogs, how-to explainers, comparison videos, Shorts and live streams, each with a worked example you can adapt by changing the topic, the result and the links to your own. Copy the structure, never the wording, and always read the finished description back as a viewer who has not seen the video yet. Most YouTube descriptions fail in the same quiet way. They are written last, in a hurry, after the video is edited and the title is chosen, and they read like an afterthought: a single vague sentence, a pile of links, and a row of tags copied from the last video. The viewer who opens the description — or who reads the first lines under the title before deciding whether to watch — learns almost nothing from it, and so the description does no work at all. That is a wasted opportunity, because the description is one of the few places on YouTube where the creator gets to speak directly to a viewer who is still deciding. The title made a promise and the thumbnail made an impression, but the description is where the viewer checks whether the promise is real: what will I learn, is this video for someone like me, and is it worth my time right now? A description that answers those three questions, in plain words, in its first lines, will earn clicks that a vague description never will. The examples in this guide are built to show exactly how that answer looks, video type by video type, so you can see the pattern in action rather than being told about it in the abstract. Each example is a complete, worked description for a made-up video on a clear topic, written the way it would appear under a real video. The topics are invented, but the structure is the point: the first lines, the promise, the detail, the chapters, the links and the next step, in an order that a deciding viewer can follow in seconds. Read the examples for your own kind of video first, then read the rest, because the pattern is the same throughout and the differences — what a review needs that a vlog does not, what a tutorial needs that a comparison does not — are where the craft lives.

The pattern every example follows

Before the examples, it helps to name the pattern they all share, because once you can see it you will recognise it in every example that follows and you will be able to build your own descriptions without copying any of them. The pattern has five parts, in this order. First, the promise: one or two sentences that say what the video delivers and who it is for, in the words the viewer would use. Second, the proof or the detail: a short passage that makes the promise believable — what is covered, what is tested, what is shown, or what the viewer will be able to do afterwards. Third, the structure: chapters or a short list of what happens in the video, so the viewer can see the shape of it and jump to the part they need. Fourth, the links: the resources mentioned in the video, the tools or products used, and the places to follow the creator, grouped under short headings and labelled honestly. Fifth, the next step: one clear action for the viewer who liked the video — watch the next video, try the method, or leave a comment answering a question. That order matters. A viewer who is deciding reads the first lines and stops there if the promise is unclear, so the promise must come before the links, the chapters and the background. A viewer who has already decided to watch may read the whole description, but they read it after watching, looking for the link or the chapter they need — so the links and chapters must be easy to find further down. Writing in that order serves both readers at once. The pattern also explains why the worst descriptions fail: they start with links, or with a biography, or with a list of tags, and the promise — the one thing the deciding viewer needed — is nowhere to be found. How to adapt an example without copying it

Adapting an example is a short job, and it is worth doing properly rather than pasting the example and changing one word. Start by reading the example for your video type and writing down, in your own words, the five parts: what is your video's promise, what detail makes it believable, what are its chapters, what links does it genuinely need, and what is the one next step? Then write the description from those five notes, in your own voice, as if you were explaining the video to one viewer in person. Keep the example open beside you while you write, not to copy from but to check against: does your first line name the result as clearly as the example's does, does your detail answer the same kind of doubt, does your next step ask for one thing rather than three? Then read the finished description back twice. Read it once as a viewer who has not seen the video and does not know you: would you know what the video delivers, and would you click? Read it once as a viewer who has just watched the whole video and wants the link you mentioned: can you find it in seconds? If either reading fails, fix that part before publishing. A useful last check is to compare the description with the title and the thumbnail, because the three must agree. If the title promises a beginner tutorial, the description must not describe an advanced masterclass. If the thumbnail shows a result, the description must name that result in its first lines. Agreement between title, thumbnail and description is what makes a viewer trust the click — and trust, once earned, is what makes the same viewer click the next video too. The description generator can give you a first draft from your title and topic, but the adaptation step — your result, your audience, your links — is the part only you can do, and it is the part that makes the description yours.

Example 1: A tutorial description

Tutorials are the videos where the description has to work hardest, because the viewer is comparing several videos that all claim to teach the same thing. The description must say, in its first lines, what the viewer will be able to do by the end, how long it will take, and what they need before they start — because a viewer who cannot tell whether the tutorial matches their level will choose a different video rather than risk wasting their time. The example below is for a made-up beginner tutorial about setting up a small home studio for recording videos. Notice how the first lines name the result and the audience, the second paragraph names what is needed and what is covered, and the chapters let a viewer who only needs one step jump straight to it.

Worked example: tutorial

Learn how to set up a small home studio for recording videos, even if you have never recorded before. By the end of this video you will have a quiet corner that looks and sounds good on camera, using the room and the equipment you already have.

This tutorial is for beginners who want to record talking-head videos at home. You do not need a spare room or expensive equipment — a corner of a bedroom, a phone or a basic camera, and one lamp are enough to start. We cover choosing the corner, cutting echo with things you already own, placing the light so your face is lit evenly, framing the shot, and testing the sound before you record.

Chapters: Choosing your corner. Cutting the echo. Placing the light. Framing the shot. Testing the sound. Your first recording.

Links mentioned in this video: the checklist for your first setup, and the guide to choosing a microphone for your budget. Follow for a new tutorial every week, and tell me in the comments what you record in your new studio.

Watch next: the video on framing and lighting for small rooms, linked on the end screen.

What makes this example work is its honesty about the starting point. It says the viewer does not need a spare room or expensive equipment, which removes the two doubts most beginners have before they press play. It lists what is covered in plain words, so the viewer can see that the tutorial answers the questions they actually have, rather than a list of technical terms they do not yet understand. And it ends with a single next step — the comments question — rather than asking for a subscription, a like, a share and a visit to another platform all at once. If your tutorial is for a more advanced audience, the same structure still works, but the first lines must name the advanced result and the knowledge the viewer is expected to bring. A tutorial that says it is for everyone is usually for no one, because a beginner will find it too fast and an expert will find it too slow. Name the level, name the result, and let the viewer who matches both click with confidence. If the tutorial belongs to a series, say so in the description and link to the earlier videos in order, because a viewer who arrives at part three needs to know that parts one and two exist before they can follow it.

Example 2: A product review description

A review description has a different job from a tutorial description: the viewer is not asking what they will learn, but whether they can trust this review. The description must say what was tested, how it was tested, and what the reviewer is not claiming — because a review that reads like an advert will be passed over, while a review that states its method plainly will be trusted. The example below is for a made-up review of a budget microphone, written for creators choosing their first microphone. Notice that the first lines name the product, the question the review answers, and the test the reviewer actually did, rather than a verdict on its own.

Worked example: product review

Is this budget microphone good enough for your first YouTube videos? I used it for two weeks of real recordings — voiceovers, video calls and outdoor clips — and this review shows the recordings, the problems I found, and who should and should not buy it.

This is an honest review, not a sponsored video. The microphone was bought with my own money, and no one paid for the verdict. I tested the sound in a quiet room, in a room with background noise, and outside, and I compare it with the microphone I used before, so you can hear the difference for yourself.

Chapters: What is in the box. Setting it up. Quiet room test. Background noise test. Outdoor test. Who it suits, and who it does not. The verdict.

Links: the microphone, the stand and cable I used, and the full written notes from the tests. If you buy through the links, I may earn a small commission at no extra cost to you — it helps the channel and it does not change the verdict.

Have you used this microphone? Tell me in the comments how it sounded for you, and watch the comparison video next if you are choosing between two models.

The strength of this example is that it answers the trust question before the verdict question. A viewer who reads the first lines knows the review is based on two weeks of real use, knows what was tested, and knows the reviewer paid for the product — and that makes the verdict, when it comes in the video, worth hearing. The disclosure about commission links is placed with the links themselves, in plain words, rather than buried at the bottom, which is how a viewer expects an honest creator to handle it. The description also names who the product does not suit, which is the sentence most review descriptions leave out and the sentence that does the most for trust: a reviewer who will say "do not buy this if" is a reviewer whose "buy this if" can be believed. If your review covers a product you were given, say so in the first lines, not only in the video, because a viewer who learns it later will doubt everything else the description said. And if your review compares several products, name all of them in the description, so the viewer searching for one of them can find your video at all. Example 3: A vlog description

Vlogs are the hardest descriptions to write well, because the video itself is often a loose collection of moments rather than a single promise — and a description that tries to list every moment becomes a list nobody reads. The answer is to find the thread: the one journey, question or change that holds the day together, and to name that thread in the first lines, with the moments listed after it as chapters. The example below is for a made-up vlog about a creator's first week working from a new city. Notice that the first lines name the move and the question the week will answer, and the chapters name the moments without trying to explain each one.

Worked example: vlog

My first week working from a new city — finding a desk, a routine, and a coffee place that opens early enough. This vlog follows the week from the first morning to the first finished video, including the parts that did not go to plan.

I moved for a change of pace and a lower cost of living, and I wanted to know whether I could do the same work, just as well, from a smaller flat in a place where I knew no one. This video is the honest answer: the good days, the lonely evening, the workspace that worked, and the routine I kept.

Chapters: Arrival and the first morning. The flat and the desk setup. Finding somewhere to work. The day nothing went to plan. The routine that stuck. What I would do differently.

Links: the desk and the bag I mention, and the playlist of the moving and settling-in videos. Follow for a new vlog every week, and tell me in the comments where you work best — home, a cafe, or somewhere else entirely.

What makes this example work is that it gives the vlog a shape before the viewer presses play. A vlog described as "a week in my life" could be about anything, and a viewer with limited time will not gamble on anything. A vlog described as a first week in a new city, answering whether the move worked, is a story with a question and an ending, and the viewer can decide in seconds whether they want to hear the answer. The description also does a quiet piece of honesty work: it says the video includes the parts that did not go to plan, which tells the viewer this is not a polished advert for a new life but a real week, with the awkward parts left in. That honesty is what vlog viewers come back for, and the description is the first place they look for it. If your vlog has no single thread, the description should say what the video is for instead: a calm watch, a behind-the-scenes look at a project, a day of errands with the creator. Name the feeling or the purpose, rather than listing the moments, and the right viewer will recognise the video they wanted. And if the vlog mentions places, products or people, link them and name them in the description, because viewers will ask in the comments if you do not — and a description that answers the questions before they are asked is a description that keeps the comments about the video itself.

Example 4: An explainer and how-to description

Explainers and how-to videos sit between tutorials and reviews: the viewer wants an answer to a question, and the description must show that the video answers that exact question, at the right depth, without wasting their time. The example below is for a made-up explainer about why videos get demonetised, written for small creators who have just had it happen to them. Notice that the first lines name the question, the audience and the answer's shape — the reasons, the checks, and what to do next — so the viewer knows this is a practical explainer rather than a complaint about the platform.

Worked example: explainer

Why was your video demonetised, and what can you do about it? This video explains the common reasons in plain language, shows how to check which reason applies to your video, and walks through the steps to take next — for small creators, without jargon.

If you have just seen a yellow icon on a video you worked hard on, this video is for you. We cover what the icon means, the reasons it appears most often, how to review your own video for the likely cause, and what happens when you request a review. No promises are made about any single video, because every case is checked on its own facts.

Chapters: What the icon means. The common reasons. Checking your own video. Requesting a review. Preventing it next time.

Links: the platform's own help pages on monetisation, and the checklist from this video to work through before you upload. Tell me in the comments which reason applied to your video, if you found out — it helps other creators reading the comments too.

The lesson of this example is restraint. An explainer description that promises to reveal a secret, or to guarantee an outcome, will be clicked and then abandoned when the video cannot deliver — and the abandonment matters, because a viewer who leaves quickly tells the platform the video did not satisfy them. This example promises something smaller and truer: an explanation in plain language, a way to check, and the steps to take next. That is a promise the video can keep, and a viewer who gets what was promised will watch to the end and trust the next video from the same creator. The description also names what it will not do — it makes no promise about any single video — which is the kind of honesty that reads as confidence rather than weakness. If your explainer covers a topic that changes often, the description should say when the video was made and point the viewer to the platform's own current help pages, because advice about a changing system goes out of date and a description that admits it will age better than one that pretends it will not. And if your explainer answers a question people ask in search, use the exact question as the first line, because the viewer who typed that question will recognise their own words and click the video that answers them in their own language.

Example 5: A comparison description

Comparison videos are chosen from a list of similar videos, so the description must do the comparison's selling in miniature: name the things being compared, name the question the comparison answers, and name the basis of the comparison, so the viewer can tell this comparison is fair and complete. The example below compares two made-up video editing tools for beginners. Notice that the first lines name both tools, the viewer, and the decision the video helps with — and that the chapters are organised by the questions a buyer asks, not by the tools' feature lists.

Worked example: comparison

Tool A or Tool B for editing your first YouTube videos? This comparison tests both on the same beginner project — the same clips, the same time limit, the same export — and shows which one is easier to learn, which one is faster, and which one suits which kind of creator.

Both tools were tested on a real project, not on their feature lists. I edited the same short video in each one, timed the main tasks, and noted where each tool helped and where it got in the way. Prices are given as they were on the day of recording, and may have changed since — check the links for the current price before you buy.

Chapters: The project and the test. Learning the basics. Editing speed. Exporting and sharing. What each tool does best. Which one to choose, and why.

Links: both tools, the project files used in the test, and the full written comparison. Buying through the links may earn a small commission, which does not affect the comparison.

Which tool do you use, and what made you choose it? Tell me in the comments, and watch the beginner editing tutorial next if you are starting from scratch.

What makes this example trustworthy is the test. A comparison that only lists features is a comparison the viewer could have read on the tools' own websites, and it gives them no reason to watch. A comparison that tests both tools on the same project, under the same conditions, gives the viewer something they cannot get anywhere else: a fair, observed difference. The description says what the test was, in one sentence, and that sentence does the work of the whole video's credibility. The note about prices is equally important, and it is the detail most comparison descriptions get wrong: prices change, and a description that states a price as a permanent fact will be wrong within months and will mislead every viewer who reads it afterwards. Saying the price was correct on the day of recording, and pointing the viewer to the link for the current price, keeps the description honest for as long as the video exists. If your comparison has a clear winner, the description can say so — but it should also say for whom, because the best tool for one kind of creator is rarely the best for every kind, and a verdict that names its audience is a verdict a viewer can act on. Example 6: A Shorts description

Shorts descriptions are read differently from long-form descriptions, because the viewer meets a Short in a feed, on a phone, with the next video one swipe away. The description is rarely read before watching — the video itself is the pitch — but it is read afterwards, by the viewer who wants the link, the next part, or the full video the Short came from. A Shorts description should therefore be very short, and every line should earn its place: the result in one line, the link to the full video or the next part, and one next step. The example below is for a made-up Short showing one tip from a longer tutorial. Notice that it says nothing the video does not say, and that the link to the full video is the first thing after the result.

Worked example: Short

One tip that makes a home recording sound clearer: move the microphone closer, and the room disappears. Full tutorial linked below.

Watch the full home studio tutorial for the whole setup, and tell me in the comments what your recordings sound like now.

The temptation with a Short is to write a long description, because the video is short and the creator feels the description should carry the missing detail. Resist it. A long description under a Short will not be read in the feed, and it will not help the Short be found in the way a long-form description helps a long-form video, because Shorts are found mainly through the feed rather than through search. What the Shorts description can do is convert the viewer who liked the Short: send them to the full video, to the next part, or to the playlist where the series lives. That is the whole job, and two or three lines do it. Keep the searchable words in the Short's title and in the spoken words of the video itself, rather than in a block of text under it, and keep the description for the viewer who has already watched and wants more. If the Short is part of a series, number it in the description and link to the playlist, so a viewer who finds part three can find parts one and two without searching. If the Short stands alone, link to the one video that continues it, and only that one — a Shorts description with five links is a description the viewer will not read at all. Example 7: A live stream and event description

A live stream description is written for two audiences at two different times: the viewer deciding whether to attend or set a reminder before the stream, and the viewer who finds the recording afterwards. The description must therefore say when the stream happens, what will happen in it, and what the viewer should bring or prepare — and it must still read well after the event, when the time has passed. The example below is for a made-up live question-and-answer session about starting a channel. Notice that the first lines name the event, the date and time in plain words, and the question the session answers, and that the description tells the viewer how to take part rather than only telling them to watch.

Worked example: live stream

Live question and answer: starting a YouTube channel in 2026. Join live to ask your question, or leave it in the comments beforehand and I will answer it on the stream.

This session is for new creators who have not started yet, or who started recently and feel stuck. We will cover choosing a topic, making the first videos with what you have, and what to do when the first videos get few views. Bring your question — the chat will be open throughout, and the questions left in the comments before the stream will be answered first.

Chapters will be added after the stream, so you can jump to the question you came for. The recording will stay up after the event.

Links: the beginner guides mentioned on the stream, and the playlist for new creators. Subscribe and turn on notifications if you want to be told when the next live session starts.

The key detail in this example is the instruction for taking part. A live stream is the one kind of video where the viewer is not only watching but can join in, and a description that only describes the topic wastes that difference. Telling the viewer to leave a question in advance gives the shy viewer a way in, gives the stream its first questions before it starts, and gives the recording a set of questions that later viewers will recognise as their own. The note that chapters will be added afterwards is equally practical: it tells the later viewer the recording will be navigable, which is the main reason a viewer will watch a long recording at all. If the stream has a guest, name the guest and their topic in the first lines, because the guest is often the reason a viewer attends. If the stream covers a topic that needs preparation — a worksheet, a file, a question to think about — link it in the description and name it in the first paragraph, so the viewer can prepare before the stream rather than discovering it halfway through. And after the stream, update the description: add the chapters, remove the reminder language, and say plainly that this is the recording, so the description serves the second audience as well as it served the first.

Mistakes that waste a good description

  • Starting with links or a biography — the viewer who is deciding never reaches the promise, because the promise is buried below the links.
  • Writing one vague sentence — "new video, hope you like it" tells the viewer nothing about the video, and nothing about why they should watch it now.
  • Copying another creator's wording — the description describes the wrong video, promises the wrong result, and reads as generic the moment it is compared with the video itself.
  • Stuffing the same phrase in again and again — the description becomes unpleasant to read, and the repetition does not make the video more findable.
  • Listing links without labels — the viewer does not know what will open, so they click none of them.
  • Promising a result the video does not deliver — the viewer leaves quickly, and the next video from the same creator starts with less trust.
  • Using the same description for every video — a copied description tells the viewer the creator did not think this video was worth describing, and the viewer agrees.
  • Putting the next step last, or leaving it out — the viewer finishes the description and does nothing, because the description never asked for one thing.
  • Stating prices or platform rules as permanent facts — prices change and rules are updated, and a description that states them as facts will mislead every later viewer.

A description is not a chore to be finished after the video. It is the last piece of the video's packaging, and it is the piece that speaks to the viewer who is still deciding. Write it before you upload, not after, and write it for the two readers it will have: the viewer who has not watched yet and needs the promise, and the viewer who has watched and needs the link. Give the first reader the result, the audience and the detail that makes the promise believable, in the first lines. Give the second reader chapters they can jump to and links they can find in seconds, grouped and labelled. Keep the two readers in mind and the description will do its job without any tricks: it will help the right viewer find the right video, and it will help that viewer take the next step the video was made for. Frequently asked questions

How long should a YouTube description be?

Long enough to say what the video is, who it is for, and what the viewer should do next — and no longer than that. The first two or three lines do the most work, because they are the lines a viewer sees before clicking to read more, so put the promise of the video there. The rest of the description can carry chapters, links and extra detail for the viewers who want it. A short description that says the promise clearly will beat a long description that buries it every time.

Should I copy a description template word for word?

No. Copy the structure, not the words. The examples in this guide work because each one names a specific result, a specific audience and a specific next step — and those details are different for every video. If you paste another creator's wording, the description will describe the wrong video, promise the wrong result, and read as generic to anyone who compares it with the video itself. Take the shape of the example, fill it with your own topic, your own result and your own links, and read it back as a viewer before you publish.

Where should links go in a YouTube description?

Put the link that matters most near the top, after the first lines, so a viewer who is ready to act does not have to hunt for it. Then group the remaining links further down, under clear short headings, so each kind of link is easy to find: the video or resource mentioned in the video, the tools or products used, and the places to follow or contact you. Label every link honestly, so the viewer knows what will open before they click it. A description full of unlabelled links looks like a sales page and will be ignored, while a short list of labelled links looks helpful and gets used.

Do keywords in the description help a video get found?

The words in a description help YouTube understand what a video is about, which is useful, but stuffing the same phrase in again and again does not help and makes the description unpleasant to read. Write the description in the words a viewer would use to describe the video, use the main topic phrase once or twice where it fits naturally, and let the title, the description and the video itself agree with each other. The examples in this guide use the topic words naturally, because each example is about one clear topic. That agreement between title, description and content is worth more than any list of keywords pasted at the bottom.
